\NoteTitle{5}{3}{Neighbourhood methods: nearest\\ neighbours, Parzen windows and distances}{Neighbourhood methods}{%
Neighbourhood methods predict at a point by letting nearby training examples vote. They need no training, make no
assumption about the shape of the target function, and handle classification and regression with one
weighted-average formula, whose denominator --- the total weight near the query --- is itself a density estimate. This note develops $k$-nearest neighbours and Parzen windows (kernel smoothing and kernel density
estimation), discusses the distances they rely on, and shows that the same ideas power retrieval in today's AI
systems and the attention mechanism of transformers. A small dataset of eight points is followed through every
method by hand; each algorithm is given as pseudocode with its running time. Model selection follows Note~4; the
geometry of high dimension follows Note~3.}

\section{Let the neighbours vote}\label{sec:knn}

Fix a distance $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ on the input space, Euclidean unless stated otherwise. For a query $\x$, let
$N_k(\x)$ be the set of indices of the $k$ training points closest to $\x$. The \emph{$k$-nearest-neighbour} ($k$-NN)
prediction averages their targets. All three tasks fit one formula,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted}
  \bar{\vect{y}}(\x) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\x)\, \vect{t}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\x)}, \qquad
  w_i(\x) = \ind{i \in N_k(\x)},
\end{equation}
with a task-dependent target encoding $\vect{t}_i$ and read-out:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{regression}: $\vect{t}_i = y_i$ and $f(\x) = \bar{\vect{y}}(\x)$, the mean target of the neighbours
(with several real outputs, $\vect{t}_i$ is the vector of targets and the average is taken component by component);
\item \textbf{classification with $m$ classes}: $\vect{t}_i = \mathrm{onehot}(y_i) \in \{0,1\}^m$, so
$\bar{\vect{y}}(\x)$ is the vector of class frequencies among the neighbours --- an estimate of $P(Y = \cdot \mid
\X = \x)$ --- and $f(\x) = \argmax_c \bar y_c(\x)$, the majority vote;
\item \textbf{binary classification} with $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$: $f(\x) = \operatorname{sign} \bar y(\x)$.
\end{itemize}
Like the histograms of Note~3, $k$-NN replaces $\E[Y \mid \X = \x]$ by an average over a neighbourhood, but the
neighbourhood is now centred on the query and adapts its size to the local density of data: small where examples are
plentiful, large where they are sparse. No cell is ever empty.

Neighbourhood methods are called \emph{non-parametric}: they assume nothing about the form of $f$, and their
``parameters'' are the training set itself, which must be stored. Their hyperparameters --- $k$ and the distance ---
are chosen on validation data as in Note~4. They are also called \emph{lazy} (or memory-based) learners: all the work
is postponed until a query arrives.

\subsection{One formula, three read-outs}\label{sec:oneformula}

Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted} looks more general than ``average the neighbours'', so let us check that it is the same
thing. Exactly $k$ of the weights equal one and the others are zero, so $\sum_i w_i(\x) = k$ and
\[
  \bar{\vect{y}}(\x) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in N_k(\x)} \vect{t}_i .
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Regression.} The mean is not an arbitrary choice: it is the constant $c$ that fits the neighbours best in
squared error. Setting the derivative of $J(c) = \sum_{i \in N_k(\x)} (y_i - c)^2$ to zero,
\[
  J'(c) = -2 \sum_{i \in N_k(\x)} (y_i - c) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\x)} y_i .
\]
This is the local, finite-sample version of the statement of Note~1, Section~4, that $\E[Y \mid \X = \x]$ minimises
the expected squared error.
\item \emph{Classification.} Component $c$ of the average of one-hot vectors is $\bar y_c = k_c / k$, where $k_c$ is the
number of neighbours of class $c$. These numbers are non-negative and sum to one, so they form a probability vector,
our estimate $\hat P(Y = c \mid \X = \x) = k_c/k$. The $\argmax$ picks the most frequent class, which is also the
constant prediction that misclassifies the fewest neighbours.
\item \emph{Binary classification with $\pm 1$ labels.} With $k_+$ positive and $k_- = k - k_+$ negative
neighbours, $\bar y = (k_+ - k_-)/k = (2k_+ - k)/k$, which is positive exactly when $k_+ > k/2$: the sign rule is the
majority vote again, and $(1 + \bar y)/2 = k_+/k$ is the estimated probability of the positive class.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ties}\label{sec:ties}

Two kinds of ties occur, and an implementation must decide both.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Distance ties}: several points are at the same distance as the $k$-th neighbour. In the example, $\x_4$ and
$\x_5$ are both at squared distance 5, so ``the four nearest neighbours'' is not well defined. Common conventions
are to keep the point with the smaller index (what a stable sort does) or to include all tied points, letting the
neighbourhood grow slightly.
\item \emph{Vote ties}: two classes receive the same number of votes. With $k = 4$ in the example, whichever of
$\x_4$, $\x_5$ is kept, the votes are A\,:\,B $= 2 : 2$.
\end{itemize}
For two classes an odd $k$ prevents vote ties; with $m \ge 3$ classes no $k > 1$ does (two classes can tie at
$k = 2$, three can share $k = 3$ votes equally). Three common rules for vote ties, applied to $k = 4$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{drop the farthest neighbour} and vote again with $k - 1$, repeating if needed: here $k = 3$ gives B;
\item \emph{prefer the tied class with the nearest member}: $\x_1$ (A) is nearer than any B point, so A;
\item \emph{weight the votes by inverse distance}, $w_i = 1/\norm{\x_i - \x}$ for $i \in N_k(\x)$: A collects
$1/1 + 1/\sqrt{5} = 1.447$ and B collects $2/\sqrt{2} = 1.414$, so A.
\end{enumerate}
Different rules give different answers on a genuinely ambiguous query; what matters is to fix one and use it
consistently. Rule~1 is the one used in Algorithms~\ref{alg:knn} and~\ref{alg:knnloo} below. Rule~3, distance-weighted
$k$-NN (a one-line change of Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn}), is a first step towards the soft weights of
Section~\ref{sec:parzen}.

is made of pieces of perpendicular bisectors between differently labelled neighbours (Figure~\ref{fig:voronoi}). It
can be arbitrarily complex: one mislabelled point carves out its own island.

\paragraph{The choice of $k$} Figure~\ref{fig:knnk} shows $k$-NN on 300 points from two interleaved half-moons.
With $k = 1$ the training error is zero and the boundary follows every noisy point; the test error is 9.2\%. With
$k = 15$ the boundary is smooth and training and test errors agree at about 6\%. With $k = 151$, half the training set
votes everywhere, the boundary is too simple to follow the moons and both errors rise to about 19\%. Small $k$ means
low bias and high variance; large $k$ the reverse (Note~4, Section~6). In the limit $k = n$ every query receives the
overall majority class (and, in regression, the overall mean target).

\paragraph{What theory guarantees} With enough data, nearest neighbours are close to the query and the method
approaches the ideal. As $n \to \infty$, the error of $1$-NN is at most twice the Bayes error (for two classes,
$R^* \le R_{1\text{-NN}} \le 2R^*(1 - R^*)$)~\cite{cover1967}, and $k$-NN is \emph{consistent} --- its risk converges to
the Bayes risk for every distribution --- if $k \to \infty$ while $k/n \to 0$~\cite{stone1977}. The two conditions
have a plain meaning: $k \to \infty$ makes the vote an average over many points (variance $\to 0$), and $k/n \to 0$
makes the neighbourhood shrink around $\x$ (bias $\to 0$). The catch is the rate: in $d$ dimensions the distance to
the nearest neighbour shrinks only like $n^{-1/d}$ --- a ball of radius $r$ around $\x$ holds about $n r^d$ of the
points (up to a constant), and setting this equal to 1 gives $r \sim n^{-1/d}$ --- and Note~3, Section~3, showed what
that means for $d = 100$.

\section{Computing neighbours}\label{sec:compute}

The algorithm is short (Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn}). Training stores $X \in \R^{n \times d}$ and the targets. Prediction
computes the distances from the query to all training points, selects the $k$ smallest and reads out the vote or the
mean.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{$k$-nearest neighbours, brute force: classification and regression}
\label{alg:knn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training inputs $\x_1, \dots, \x_n \in \R^d$, targets $y_1, \dots, y_n$ (classes $1..m$ or real numbers),
number of neighbours $k \le n$
\Function{Train}{$\Dtrain$}
  \State store $X$ and $\vect{y}$ \Comment{nothing is fitted}
\EndFunction
\Function{Neighbours}{$\x, k$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $\delta_i \gets \norm{\x_i - \x}^2$ \Comment{squared distance: same ranking, no square root}
  \EndFor
  \State $N \gets$ indices of the $k$ smallest $\delta_i$, sorted by $\delta_i$ \Comment{partial selection}
  \State \Return $N$
\EndFunction
\Function{Classify}{$\x$}
  \State $(i_1, \dots, i_k) \gets \Call{Neighbours}{\x, k}$
  \State $v_c \gets 0$ for $c = 1, \dots, m$; \ $\hat c \gets$ none \Comment{$\hat c$: current leader}
  \For{$r = 1, \dots, k$}
    \State $v_{y_{i_r}} \gets v_{y_{i_r}} + 1$ \Comment{weighted variant: add $1/\norm{\x_{i_r} - \x}$ (rule 3)}
    \State \textbf{if} $\hat c$ is none \textbf{or} $v_{y_{i_r}} > v_{\hat c}$ \textbf{then} $\hat c \gets y_{i_r}$
    \Comment{strict ``$>$'': tie rule 1}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\hat c$ \Comment{$v_c/k$ estimates $P(Y = c \mid \X = \x)$}
\EndFunction
\Function{Regress}{$\x$}
  \State $N \gets \Call{Neighbours}{\x, k}$
  \State \Return $\frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in N} y_i$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The leader $\hat c$ changes only when a class strictly overtakes it, so among classes tied at the end it keeps the one
that reached the winning count first. This is exactly tie rule~1 of Section~\ref{sec:ties}. Indeed, the leader always
holds a maximal count, and it changes only at a step where another class becomes the \emph{unique} maximum. Let
$r \le k$ be the last step at which a single class holds the maximal count (step 1 always qualifies). At step $r$ that
class is the leader, and since no class becomes the unique maximum afterwards, the leader never changes again. Tie
rule~1 drops the farthest neighbours until the tie disappears, which takes it back to the same step $r$ and the same
class. In the weighted variant (rule~3) the counts $v_c$ become sums of inverse distances; a neighbour at distance
zero is handled first by returning its class directly. In numpy, a batch of queries is handled with the distance matrix of
Note~1, Section~6:

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{verbatim}
def knn_classify(Xtr, ytr, Xq, k, m):        # ytr: integer labels 0..m-1
    D = sq_distances(Xq, Xtr)                 # (n_q, n), Note 1
    idx = np.argpartition(D, k - 1, axis=1)[:, :k]   # k nearest, unordered
    votes = np.eye(m)[ytr[idx]].sum(axis=1)   # (n_q, m) class counts
    return votes.argmax(axis=1)               # ties: smallest class index
\end{verbatim}
\end{minipage}

Partial selection (\texttt{argpartition}, ``quickselect'') finds the $k$ smallest of $n$ numbers in $O(n)$ average
time; a full sort would cost $O(n \log n)$, and a heap of size $k$ costs $O(n \log k)$. Note that this short version
breaks vote ties by class index, a different (and cruder) convention than Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn}.

\begin{runtime}{$k$-NN, brute force (Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn})}
Notation: $n$ training examples, $d$ features, $m$ classes, $k$ neighbours, $n_q$ queries.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Training}: $O(1)$, or $O(nd)$ to copy the data. Nothing is computed.
\item \emph{Prediction per query}: $O(nd)$ for the $n$ distances (each a sum of $d$ squares), $O(n)$ for the
selection (plus $O(k \log k)$ to sort the $k$ winners), $O(k + m)$ for the vote or the mean. Total
$O(nd + k\log k + m)$, which is $O(nd + m)$ in the usual case $k \log k \le nd$: the distances dominate.
\item \emph{Memory}: $O(nd)$ for the stored training set, $O(n)$ working space per query; a batch of $n_q$ queries
needs the $n_q \times n$ distance matrix, so large batches are processed in chunks.
\item \emph{Distance-weighted variant} (rule~3): $k$ extra square roots and divisions, so the cost is unchanged.
\item \emph{In practice}: the $O(n_q nd)$ distance computation is one matrix product (Note~1, Section~6), which BLAS
libraries and GPUs execute at close to peak speed. Still, with $n = 10^6$ and $d = 100$, one query costs $10^8$
multiply--adds and the stored data take 400\,MB in 32-bit floats: training is free, prediction is expensive --- the
opposite of most models.
\end{itemize}
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Choosing \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} by leave-one-out}\label{sec:choosek}

Any of the validation schemes of Note~4, Section~4, can select $k$. For $k$-NN, leave-one-out cross-validation is
unusually cheap. Leaving $\x_i$ out and predicting it from the rest only requires the neighbours of $\x_i$ among the
other points, i.e.\ its own row of the distance matrix $D$ with the diagonal entry $D_{ii}$ removed. Moreover, once
the $k_{\max}$ nearest neighbours of $\x_i$ are sorted, the predictions for \emph{all} $k = 1, \dots, k_{\max}$ follow by walking down
the list once and updating the running vote (Algorithm~\ref{alg:knnloo}). One pass gives the whole validation curve.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Leave-one-out choice of $k$ for $k$-NN classification}
\label{alg:knnloo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training set $\{(\x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with classes $1..m$; largest candidate $k_{\max} \le n - 1$
\Ensure the $k \in \{1, \dots, k_{\max}\}$ with the smallest leave-one-out error, and all the error estimates
\State $e_k \gets 0$ for $k = 1, \dots, k_{\max}$
\For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
  \State $\delta_j \gets \norm{\x_j - \x_i}^2$ for all $j$; \ $\delta_i \gets \infty$ \Comment{leave $\x_i$ out}
  \State $(j_1, \dots, j_{k_{\max}}) \gets$ indices of the $k_{\max}$ smallest $\delta_j$, sorted
  \State $v_c \gets 0$ for all $c$; \ $\hat c \gets$ none
  \For{$r = 1, \dots, k_{\max}$} \Comment{prediction with $k = r$ neighbours}
    \State $v_{y_{j_r}} \gets v_{y_{j_r}} + 1$
    \State \textbf{if} $\hat c$ is none \textbf{or} $v_{y_{j_r}} > v_{\hat c}$ \textbf{then} $\hat c \gets y_{j_r}$
    \State $e_r \gets e_r + \ind{\hat c \ne y_i}$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $k^* = \argmin_k e_k$ and the error estimates $e_k / n$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{leave-one-out choice of $k$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:knnloo})}
For each of the $n$ points: $O(nd)$ distances, $O(n + k_{\max} \log k_{\max})$ selection and sorting, $O(k_{\max})$
for the running vote. Total $O(n^2 d + nk_{\max}\log k_{\max})$ time --- the cost of predicting the $n$ training points
once --- for the whole curve $k = 1, \dots, k_{\max}$. Memory $O(nd + n + k_{\max})$ if rows are computed one at a
time. Without the running vote, trying each $k$ separately would multiply the cost by $k_{\max}$. For $n$ beyond about $10^5$ the $n^2$ factor bites, and a single
validation split (cost $O(n_{\mathrm{val}}\, n d)$) or approximate neighbours are used instead.
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Exact search with \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}-d trees}\label{sec:kdtree}

\noindent\begin{minipage}[c]{0.62\linewidth}
In low dimension, most of the $n$ distance computations are wasted: points far from the query cannot be neighbours.
A \emph{$k$-d tree}~\cite{bentley1975,friedman1977} (``$k$-dimensional tree''; in our notation that $k$ is $d$, unrelated to
the number of neighbours) organises the
training points so that whole groups can be skipped. Each node splits its points at the median of one coordinate,
cycling through the coordinates with depth; the leaves are small boxes holding a few points
(Figure~\ref{fig:kdtree}). To find the nearest neighbour, descend to the leaf containing the query, take the best
point found there as a candidate, and walk back up: the far side of a split need only be searched if the ball around
the query whose radius is the current best distance crosses the splitting plane (Algorithm~\ref{alg:kdtree}).
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.34\linewidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/n5_kdtree.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{A $k$-d tree on 24 points, leaf size 2. The ball of radius the best distance in the
query's leaf crosses one splitting line: both shaded cells are searched, and the true nearest neighbour (circled)
lies in the other one.}
\label{fig:kdtree}
\end{minipage}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{$k$-d tree: construction and nearest-neighbour search}
\label{alg:kdtree}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Build}{$P$, depth} \Comment{$P$: a set of training points; leaf size $b$}
  \If{$|P| \le b$} \Return \Call{Leaf}{$P$} \EndIf
  \State $j \gets \text{depth} \bmod d$ \Comment{or the coordinate of largest spread}
  \State $P_L \gets$ the $\lfloor |P|/2 \rfloor$ points with smallest $x_j$, $P_R \gets$ the rest
  \Comment{$O(|P|)$ by selection}
  \State $s \gets$ midway between the largest $x_j$ in $P_L$ and the smallest in $P_R$
  \Comment{rank split: no empty side}
  \State $L \gets \Call{Build}{P_L, \text{depth} + 1}$; \ $R \gets \Call{Build}{P_R, \text{depth} + 1}$
  \State \Return \Call{Node}{$j, s, L, R$}
\EndFunction
\Function{Search}{node, $\vect{q}$, best} \Comment{query $\vect{q}$; best $= (\rho, \x)$: best distance so far, initially $(\infty, \text{none})$}
  \If{node is a leaf}
    \State \Return the better of best and the nearest point of the leaf \Comment{brute force on $\le b$ points}
  \EndIf
  \State $(j, s) \gets$ split coordinate and split value stored in node
  \State (near, far) $\gets$ (left, right) \textbf{if} $q_j < s$ \textbf{else} (right, left)
  \State best $\gets \Call{Search}{\text{near}, \vect{q}, \text{best}}$
  \If{$|q_j - s| < \rho$} \Comment{the ball around $\vect{q}$ crosses the plane $x_j = s$}
    \State best $\gets \Call{Search}{\text{far}, \vect{q}, \text{best}}$
  \EndIf
  \State \Return best
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The pruning test is safe because the distance from $\vect{q}$ to any point on the far side is at least
$|q_j - s|$: one coordinate difference never exceeds the full Euclidean distance. For $k > 1$ neighbours the candidate
becomes a list of the $k$ best points and $\rho$ the distance to the worst of them.

\begin{worked}{a $k$-d tree on the eight points}
\emph{Build} with leaf size $b = 2$. The root splits on $x_1$: the four smallest values ($\x_4, \x_7$ at 0, $\x_2$ at
1, $\x_1$ at 2) go left, the rest ($\x_3, \x_6$ at 3, $\x_5$ at 4, $\x_8$ at 5) go right, and $s = 2.5$. Both
children split on $x_2$ at $s = 2$ (midway between 1 and 3), giving four leaves:
$\{\x_7, \x_2\}$ and $\{\x_1, \x_4\}$ on the left, $\{\x_3, \x_8\}$ and $\{\x_5, \x_6\}$ on the right.

\emph{Search} for the query $\vect{q} = (0.5, 2.5)$. At the root $q_1 = 0.5 < 2.5$, so go left; there $q_2 = 2.5 \ge 2$,
so go to the leaf $\{\x_1, \x_4\}$: distances $\sqrt{2.5} = 1.58$ and $\sqrt{0.5} = 0.71$, so $\rho = 0.71$
(candidate $\x_4$). Back at the left node, $|q_2 - 2| = 0.5 < 0.71$: the ball crosses the line $x_2 = 2$, and the
leaf $\{\x_7, \x_2\}$ is searched (distances 2.55 and 1.58, no improvement). Back at the root, $|q_1 - 2.5| = 2 \ge
0.71$: the whole right half is pruned. Answer $\x_4$ after 4 distance computations instead of 8. The running query
$(2, 2)$ is the unlucky case: it lies on both lines $x_2 = 2$ and only 0.5 from $x_1 = 2.5$, so with $\rho = 1$ (from
$\x_1$) no branch can be pruned and all 8 distances are computed.
\end{worked}

\begin{runtime}{$k$-d tree (Algorithm~\ref{alg:kdtree})}
\emph{Building}: the tree has depth $O(\log n)$ and each level does $O(n)$ work for the median selections, so
$O(n \log n)$ time; $O(n)$ memory for the nodes on top of the $O(nd)$ data. \emph{Query}: in low dimension and for
well-spread data a query visits $O(\log n)$ nodes on average and scans a few leaves of $b$ points, so it costs about
$O(\log n + bd)$, or $O(d \log n)$ counting the distances computed on the way --- a huge gain over $O(nd)$. As $d$ grows, the ball around
the query crosses more and more splitting planes; a common rule of thumb is that the tree helps only when
$n \gg 2^d$. For $d$ of a few tens the search visits most leaves and becomes brute force with extra overhead --- the
curse of dimensionality of Note~3 seen from the algorithmic side.
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Approximate search}

Large systems therefore accept \emph{approximate} neighbours. Graph-based indices such as HNSW~\cite{malkov2020}
link each point to a few near neighbours and answer a query by walking greedily through this graph
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy}); compressed vector codes on GPUs~\cite{johnson2021} shrink the memory so that billions of
vectors fit. Empirically, building such an index costs about $O(n \log n)$ distance evaluations and a query about
$O(d \log n)$ instead of $O(nd)$ (there is no worst-case guarantee). Memory is $O(nd)$ for raw vectors, or $n$ times
the code size (a few tens of bytes) with compressed codes, plus the links of the graph (Table~\ref{tab:glance}). The
price is that a true neighbour is occasionally missed: the index returns \emph{most} of the true neighbours, a
fraction called the recall. We only describe these indices; their design and analysis go beyond this course.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Greedy search in a neighbour graph (the core step of graph-based indices)}
\label{alg:greedy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require points $\x_1, \dots, \x_n$, each linked to a few near neighbours $G(i)$; an entry point $e$; query $\vect{q}$
\State $c \gets e$
\Loop
  \State $j \gets \argmin_{i \in G(c)} \norm{\x_i - \vect{q}}$ \Comment{$|G(c)|$ distances, $O(|G(c)|\, d)$}
  \State \textbf{if} $\norm{\x_j - \vect{q}} \ge \norm{\x_c - \vect{q}}$ \textbf{then} \Return $c$
  \Comment{no neighbour is closer: stop}
  \State $c \gets j$
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{greedy graph search (Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy})}
Each step costs $O(Md)$ for a node with $M$ links; the number of steps is typically about $\log n$ in practice
(hierarchical layers help), giving roughly $O(Md \log n)$ per query. Memory: the vectors plus $O(nM)$ links.
Greedy search can stop at a local minimum that is not the true nearest neighbour, which is where the recall below one
comes from; practical indices keep a list of several candidates instead of one.
\end{runtime}

\begin{modern}{nearest neighbours inside AI systems}
Nearest-neighbour search in embedding space (Note~1, Section~3) is everywhere. \emph{Retrieval-augmented generation}
embeds a question, retrieves the nearest passages from a document collection and gives them to a language model as
context~\cite{lewis2020}; the ``vector databases'' behind such systems are approximate $k$-NN indices. Updating what
the system knows then means adding vectors to the index, not retraining the model. A language model can also be
improved without retraining by interpolating its next-token distribution with a $k$-NN estimate computed from a
datastore of contexts~\cite{khandelwal2020}: the $k$ retrieved contexts vote for their next tokens with weights
proportional to $\exp(-\text{squared distance})$, a distance-weighted $k$-NN vote with tokens as classes (the Gaussian
weights of Section~\ref{sec:soft}, restricted to $N_k(\x)$). And self-supervised vision models are often evaluated by
the accuracy of a $k$-NN classifier on their features~\cite{wu2018}: if a simple vote among neighbours works, the
representation has organised the data well.
\end{modern}

\section{Parzen windows: soft neighbourhoods}\label{sec:parzen}

\subsection{Hard windows}\label{sec:hard}

Instead of the $k$ nearest points, let every point within a fixed radius $h$ (a radius, not a standard deviation) vote: $w_i(\x) = \ind{d(\x_i, \x) < h}$
in \eqref{eq:weighted}. Equivalently $w_i(\x) = \ind{d(\x_i, \x)/h < 1}$: the distance is measured in units of the
window size. The two methods mirror each other:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & $k$-NN & hard Parzen window \\
\midrule
fixed & number of voters $k$ & radius $h$ \\
varies with $\x$ & radius (distance to the $k$-th neighbour) & number of voters \\
sparse region & large neighbourhood, still $k$ voters & few or no voters \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In sparse regions the window may be empty, and a default prediction is needed, as for histograms (Note~3,
Section~1): the overall majority class or mean, or the prediction of the single nearest neighbour.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Hard-window Parzen classifier and regressor}
\label{alg:hard}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training set $\{(\x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, radius $h > 0$, a default prediction $f_0$
\Function{Window}{$\x$}
  \State \Return $N = \{i : \norm{\x_i - \x}^2 < h^2\}$ \Comment{one pass, $O(nd)$}
\EndFunction
\Function{ClassifyHard}{$\x$}
  \State $N \gets \Call{Window}{\x}$; \ \textbf{if} $N = \emptyset$ \textbf{then} \Return $f_0$
  \Comment{e.g.\ majority class or 1-NN}
  \State $v_c \gets |\{i \in N : y_i = c\}|$ for $c = 1, \dots, m$
  \State \Return $\argmax_c v_c$ \Comment{ties: class of the nearest member of $N$ (rule 2)}
\EndFunction
\Function{RegressHard}{$\x$}
  \State $N \gets \Call{Window}{\x}$; \ \textbf{if} $N = \emptyset$ \textbf{then} \Return $f_0$
  \Comment{e.g.\ overall mean}
  \State \Return $\frac{1}{|N|} \sum_{i \in N} y_i$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{worked}{hard windows of four sizes}
On the data of Figure~\ref{fig:toy} with query $\x = (2,2)$, a point votes when $\norm{\x_i - \x} < h$, i.e.\ when
$\norm{\x_i - \x}^2 < h^2$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $h = 0.5$: $h^2 = 0.25$, and no point qualifies (the nearest has squared distance 1). The window is empty and
the default rule decides.
\item $h = 1.5$: $h^2 = 2.25$, voters $\{1, 2, 3\}$, the same as for $k = 3$: class B, regression $10/3 \approx 3.33$.
\item $h = 2.5$: $h^2 = 6.25$, voters $\{1, \dots, 5\}$, the same as for $k = 5$: class A, regression $4$.
\item $h = 3$: $h^2 = 9$ also admits $\x_7$ (class B, $y_7 = 1$): votes $3:3$, a tie, which tie rule~2 of
Section~\ref{sec:ties} resolves in favour of the class of the nearest member, $\x_1$: A. Regression $21/6 = 3.5$.
\end{itemize}
At a different query the same $h$ could collect a different number of points; with $k$-NN the number is fixed.
\end{worked}


\subsection{Soft windows and the Nadaraya--Watson estimator}\label{sec:soft}

Better still, let \emph{all} training points vote with weights that decrease smoothly with distance,
$w_i(\x) = K(\x_i, \x)$ for a \emph{kernel} $K$. The hard window is the special case of a ``box'' kernel. The most
common smooth choice is the Gaussian (radial basis function) kernel
\[
  K_\sigma(\x_i, \x) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\norm{\x - \x_i}^2}{2\sigma^2} \right),
\]
whose normalising constant $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}$ would cancel in the ratio \eqref{eq:weighted} and is therefore
omitted. Regression with these weights,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nw}
  f(\x) = \frac{\sum_i K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)\, y_i}{\sum_i K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)},
\end{equation}
is the Nadaraya--Watson estimator~\cite{nadaraya1964,watson1964}; classification uses one-hot targets and an
$\argmax$, exactly as for $k$-NN, so the score of class $c$ is
\[
  s_c(\x) = \frac{\sum_{i : y_i = c} K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)}{\sum_i K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)}, \qquad \sum_c s_c(\x) = 1 .
\]
For two classes with labels $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ the same read-out is the sign of the weighted mean label,
\[
  f(\x) = \operatorname{sign} \frac{\sum_i K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)\, y_i}{\sum_i K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)}
  = \operatorname{sign}\big(s_{+1}(\x) - s_{-1}(\x)\big) .
\]

\paragraph{Where the formula comes from} The argument of Section~\ref{sec:oneformula} carries over with weights. The
constant $c$ that best fits the data near $\x$, in a \emph{weighted} squared error, solves
\[
  \frac{d}{dc} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i (y_i - c)^2 = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n w_i (y_i - c) = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = \frac{\sum_i w_i y_i}{\sum_i w_i},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:nw}. Nadaraya--Watson is therefore a \emph{locally weighted mean}: at every query a new weighted
least-squares problem is solved, with weights centred on the query.

\paragraph{Local linear regression} Replacing the constant $c$ by a linear function centred on the query gives
\emph{local linear regression}: at each query $\x$ minimise
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\x) \big( y_i - b - \vect{\beta}\T(\x_i - \x) \big)^2
\]
over $b \in \R$ and $\vect{\beta} \in \R^d$, and predict $f(\x) = b$, the value of the local line at the query. This is
a weighted least-squares problem in $d + 1$ unknowns, solved by the normal equations of Note~8, Section~2, with the
weights inserted. It removes the first-order bias of Nadaraya--Watson, which is largest at the edges of the data,
where all neighbours lie on one side of the query. The price is a new $(d+1) \times (d+1)$ linear system for every
query: $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ per query instead of $O(nd)$. We mention it for completeness and do not use it further.

\begin{worked}{Gaussian weights and the Nadaraya--Watson prediction}
Same data and query $\x = (2,2)$, Gaussian kernel with $\sigma = 1$, so $w_i = e^{-\delta_i/2}$ with
$\delta_i = \norm{\x_i - \x}^2$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccccc@{}}
\toprule
$i$ & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
class, $y_i$ & A, 5 & B, 2 & B, 3 & A, 4 & A, 6 & A, 7 & B, 1 & B, 4 \\
$\delta_i$ & 1 & 2 & 2 & 5 & 5 & 10 & 8 & 10 \\
$w_i = e^{-\delta_i/2}$ & 0.607 & 0.368 & 0.368 & 0.082 & 0.082 & 0.007 & 0.018 & 0.007 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item Total weight $\sum_i w_i = 1.538$.
\item Classification: class A collects $0.607 + 0.082 + 0.082 + 0.007 = 0.777$, class B collects
$0.368 + 0.368 + 0.018 + 0.007 = 0.761$, so $s_{\mathrm{A}} = 0.777/1.538 = 0.505$ and $s_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.495$.
Prediction A, by a hair: the query really is on the boundary.
\item Regression: $\sum_i w_i y_i = 5(0.607) + 2(0.368) + 3(0.368) + 4(0.082) + 6(0.082) + 7(0.007) + 1(0.018) +
4(0.007) = 5.785$, so $f(\x) = 5.785 / 1.538 = 3.76$.
\end{itemize}
With $\sigma = 0.5$ the weights become $e^{-2\delta_i}$: $0.135$ for $\x_1$, $0.018$ for $\x_2$ and $\x_3$, and less
than $10^{-4}$ for the rest. Now $s_{\mathrm{A}} = 0.787$ and $f(\x) = 4.47$, close to the 1-NN answers (A and 5).
Conversely, as $\sigma \to \infty$ every weight tends to 1: $s_{\mathrm{A}} \to 4/8 = 0.5$ and $f(\x)$ tends to the mean
of all targets, $32/8 = 4$.
\end{worked}

The width $\sigma$ plays the role of $k$ (Figure~\ref{fig:parzenclass}): small widths give fragmented regions that
follow individual points, large widths give smooth boundaries that may miss structure. In the limit $\sigma \to 0$ the
nearest point dominates and the method becomes 1-NN (Question~C5); as $\sigma \to \infty$ it predicts the overall
mean or majority. For regression, Figure~\ref{fig:regression} compares the two families: $k$-NN regression is a
step function, since the neighbour set changes abruptly as the query moves, whereas the Gaussian weights change
smoothly and so does the Nadaraya--Watson curve.

\paragraph{A word on names} ``Kernel'' here means a \emph{smoothing} kernel, a non-negative bump that sets the
weights. Note~10, Section~3, uses the same word for a function $k(\x, \x')$ that computes an inner product in a feature space, and its Section~8 sets the two meanings side by side. The
Gaussian kernel is both, which is why the two ideas are often confused; the methods built on them are different.

\subsection{Computing the weights safely}\label{sec:lse}

For a query far from all data, or a small $\sigma$, every weight can underflow to zero and \eqref{eq:nw} becomes
$0/0$. Write $w_i = e^{a_i}$ with $a_i = -\norm{\x - \x_i}^2/(2\sigma^2)$ and let $a^* = \max_i a_i$. Multiplying
numerator and denominator by $e^{-a^*}$ changes nothing,
\[
  f(\x) = \frac{\sum_i e^{a_i} y_i}{\sum_i e^{a_i}} = \frac{\sum_i e^{a_i - a^*} y_i}{\sum_i e^{a_i - a^*}},
\]
but now every exponent is $\le 0$ and the largest term equals $e^0 = 1$, so the denominator is at least 1: no
overflow, no $0/0$. This is the \emph{log-sum-exp trick}; the same identity gives
$\log \sum_i e^{a_i} = a^* + \log \sum_i e^{a_i - a^*}$, used for densities in Section~\ref{sec:kde}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Gaussian Parzen classifier and Nadaraya--Watson regressor (log-sum-exp form)}
\label{alg:parzen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training set $\{(\x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, width $\sigma > 0$
\Function{Weights}{$\x$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $a_i \gets -\norm{\x - \x_i}^2 / (2\sigma^2)$ \Comment{log-weights}
  \EndFor
  \State $a^* \gets \max_i a_i$
  \State $\omega_i \gets \exp(a_i - a^*)$ for all $i$ \Comment{largest $\omega_i$ is 1}
  \State \Return $\omega$
\EndFunction
\Function{Regress}{$\x$}
  \State $\omega \gets \Call{Weights}{\x}$
  \State \Return $\sum_i \omega_i y_i \,/\, \sum_i \omega_i$
\EndFunction
\Function{Classify}{$\x$}
  \State $\omega \gets \Call{Weights}{\x}$; \ $S \gets \sum_i \omega_i$
  \State $s_c \gets 0$ for $c = 1, \dots, m$
  \State $s_{y_i} \gets s_{y_i} + \omega_i / S$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$ \Comment{scores sum to one}
  \State \Return $\argmax_c s_c$ and the scores $s$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{worked}{underflow and its cure}
With $\sigma = 0.02$ on the running example, $a_i = -\delta_i / (2 \cdot 0.02^2) = -1250\, \delta_i$, so
$a_1 = -1250$, $a_2 = a_3 = -2500$, and so on. In double precision $e^{-1250}$ is exactly $0$ (the smallest positive
double is about $e^{-744}$), so the naive formula returns $0/0$, i.e.\ \texttt{nan}. After subtracting
$a^* = -1250$ the weights are $1, e^{-1250}, \dots = 1, 0, \dots, 0$ and $f(\x) = y_1 = 5$: the 1-NN answer, which is
indeed the correct limit for a very narrow kernel.
\end{worked}


\begin{runtime}{Parzen windows (Algorithms~\ref{alg:hard} and~\ref{alg:parzen})}
\emph{Training}: none; memory $O(nd)$ for the stored data. \emph{Prediction per query}: $O(nd)$ for the distances,
$O(n)$ for the $n$ comparisons (hard window) or the $n$ exponentials (Gaussian), and $O(n + m)$ to accumulate the
class scores (each point adds to its own class). Total $O(nd + m)$, the same order as $k$-NN but without the
selection step. The log-sum-exp trick adds one pass, $O(n)$. A batch of $n_q$ queries is again one $O(n_q nd)$ matrix
product followed by element-wise operations, ideal for a GPU. For the hard window in low dimension, a $k$-d tree
answers ``all points within radius $h$'' much faster than brute force. A Gaussian kernel can be truncated at a few
$\sigma$, since $e^{-9/2} \approx 0.011$ at $3\sigma$, after which the same speed-up applies.
\end{runtime}

\begin{modern}{attention is kernel smoothing}
The attention operation at the heart of the transformer~\cite{vaswani2017} computes, for a query vector $\vect{q}$,
keys $\vect{k}_i$ and values $\vect{v}_i$ of dimension $d_k$,
\[
  \mathrm{Attention}(\vect{q}) = \sum_i \frac{\exp(\vect{q}\T\vect{k}_i / \sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_j \exp(\vect{q}\T\vect{k}_j / \sqrt{d_k})}\, \vect{v}_i ,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:nw} with an exponential kernel: each value votes with a weight set by the similarity of its key to
the query~\cite{tsai2019}. The keys play the role of the training inputs $\x_i$, the values that of the targets $y_i$.
Write $\tau = \sqrt{d_k}$ for the temperature. When queries and keys have unit norm,
$\exp(\vect{q}\T\vect{k}/\tau) = e^{1/\tau} \exp(-\norm{\vect{q} - \vect{k}}^2/(2\tau))$ is proportional to a Gaussian
kernel of width $\sigma = \sqrt{\tau}$ in $\norm{\vect{q} - \vect{k}}$; the constant $e^{1/\tau}$ cancels in the ratio
(Question~C3). The ``subtract the maximum'' trick of Section~\ref{sec:lse} is the one used in numerically
stable softmax implementations. What a transformer adds is that keys, queries and values are \emph{learned}
functions of the input, so the network learns which neighbours should vote.
\end{modern}

\section{Kernel density estimation}\label{sec:kde}

For density estimation there are no targets: each training point contributes a small bump of probability, and the
estimate is their average~\cite{rosenblatt1956,parzen1962},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kde}
  \hat p(\x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n K_h(\x - \x_i), \qquad K_h(\vect{u}) = \frac{1}{h^d}\, K\!\left(\frac{\vect{u}}{h}\right),
\end{equation}
where $K \ge 0$ is a probability density with $\int K = 1$ (the kernel ``shape'') and $h > 0$ its width, the
\emph{bandwidth}. (For discrete inputs, $K$ is a probability mass function and sums replace the integrals below.) The factor $h^{-d}$ keeps the area equal to one when the bump is stretched by $h$ in each of the $d$
directions. The standard choice is the Gaussian $K(\vect{u}) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} e^{-\norm{\vect{u}}^2/2}$, for which
$K_h(\x - \x_i) = \Normal(\x; \x_i, h^2 I)$, the isotropic Gaussian of Note~2, Section~4. The bandwidth $h$ plays the
role of the width $\sigma$ of Section~\ref{sec:soft}: $K_h(\x - \x_i)$ is $K_\sigma(\x_i, \x)$ with $\sigma = h$, now
multiplied by its normalising constant $(2\pi h^2)^{-d/2}$. (Do not confuse it with the radius $h$ of the hard window,
although that window is the special case of a ``box'' kernel.) Unlike Nadaraya--Watson, KDE is not a ratio, so here
the normalising constant matters.

\paragraph{How KDE relates to the weighted mean} Equation~\eqref{eq:kde} is not an instance of the ratio
\eqref{eq:weighted}: it is its \emph{denominator} on its own. With weights $w_i(\x) = K\big((\x - \x_i)/h\big)$,
\[
  \hat p(\x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\x) :
\]
the total weight that the data place near $\x$, normalised by the number of points and the volume of the window,
measures how dense the data are there. For the hard window, $K$ is constant on the unit ball and zero outside, and
$\hat p(\x)$ becomes the number of points within radius $h$ divided by $n$ times the volume of that ball: the
histogram density of Note~3, Section~1, with the cell centred on the query.

\paragraph{The estimate is a density} Each term is non-negative, so $\hat p \ge 0$. For the area, substitute
$\vect{u} = (\x - \x_i)/h$ in each term, so that $d\x = h^d\, d\vect{u}$:
\[
  \int K_h(\x - \x_i)\, d\x = \int \frac{1}{h^d} K\!\left(\frac{\x - \x_i}{h}\right) d\x
  = \int K(\vect{u})\, d\vect{u} = 1,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \int \hat p(\x)\, d\x = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n 1 = 1 .
\]
Unlike a histogram (Note~3, Section~1), the estimate is smooth and does not depend on where bin edges happen to fall.
The density panel of Note~1, Figure~1, was drawn this way. Because $\hat p$ is a mixture of $n$ Gaussians with equal
weights, it is also easy to \emph{sample} from: pick a training point at random and add Gaussian noise of standard
deviation $h$ (Note~2, Section~5).

\begin{worked}{a kernel density estimate by hand}
Data $x_1, \dots, x_4 = 0, 1, 3, 4$ in one dimension, Gaussian kernel, so
$\hat p(x) = \frac{1}{nh} \sum_i \varphi\big((x - x_i)/h\big)$ with $\varphi(u) = e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Useful
values: $\varphi(0) = 0.3989$, $\varphi(0.5) = 0.3521$, $\varphi(1) = 0.2420$, $\varphi(1.5) = 0.1295$,
$\varphi(2) = 0.0540$, $\varphi(4) = 0.0001$.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
$x = 1$, $h = 0.5$ & $2,\ 0,\ -4,\ -6$ & $0.4531$ & 2 & $0.227$ \\

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Gaussian kernel density estimation: log-density and sampling}
\label{alg:kde}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $\x_1, \dots, \x_n \in \R^d$, bandwidth $h > 0$
\Function{LogDensity}{$\x$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $a_i \gets -\norm{\x - \x_i}^2/(2h^2) - \frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi h^2)$ \Comment{$a_i = \log K_h(\x - \x_i)$}
  \EndFor
  \State $a^* \gets \max_i a_i$
  \State \Return $a^* + \log \sum_i \exp(a_i - a^*) - \log n$ \Comment{log-sum-exp; never $\log 0$}
\EndFunction
\Function{Sample}{}
  \State draw $i$ uniformly from $\{1, \dots, n\}$ and $\vect{\varepsilon} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, I_d)$
  \State \Return $\x_i + h\, \vect{\varepsilon}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{kernel density estimation (Algorithm~\ref{alg:kde})}
\emph{Training}: none (store the data, $O(nd)$ memory). \emph{One density evaluation}: $O(nd)$, like one $k$-NN query.
Evaluating on a grid of $G$ points costs $O(Gnd)$; in one dimension, binning the data and convolving with the kernel
by the fast Fourier transform reduces this to roughly $O(n + G \log G)$. In any dimension the $G$ evaluations form
one $G \times n$ distance matrix (one matrix product) followed by element-wise exponentials, ideal for a GPU.
\emph{One sample}: $O(d)$, independent of $n$.
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Bias, variance and the best bandwidth}\label{sec:kdebias}

The bandwidth $h$ trades bias for variance (Figure~\ref{fig:kde}). A narrow kernel puts a spike on each data point;
a wide kernel blurs the two peaks into one. More data allow a narrower kernel. The two effects can be computed.

\paragraph{The average estimate is a smoothed density} The terms $Z_i = K_h(\x - \X_i)$ in \eqref{eq:kde} are
independent and identically distributed when the data are, so $\E[\hat p(\x)] = \E[Z_1]$:
\[
  \E[\hat p(\x)] = \int K_h(\x - \vect{u})\, p(\vect{u})\, d\vect{u} = (K_h * p)(\x),
\]
the \emph{convolution} of the true density with the kernel. Whatever $n$ is, the estimator on average sees $p$
through a blurred lens of width $h$; it is unbiased only in the limit $h \to 0$, where $K_h$ approaches a delta
function.

\paragraph{Size of the bias} In one dimension, substitute $u = x - ht$ (so $du = -h\,dt$ and
$K_h(ht) = K(t)/h$). The minus sign is absorbed by swapping the limits: $u$ running from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ means
$t$ running from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$, and turning the integral back to increasing $t$ costs another minus sign.
Then expand $p$ in a Taylor series around $x$:
\[
  \E[\hat p(x)] = \int K(t)\, p(x - ht)\, dt
  = \int K(t) \Big[ p(x) - ht\, p'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2} h^2 t^2 p''(x) - \dots \Big] dt .
\]
Use $\int K = 1$, $\int t K(t)\, dt = 0$ for a symmetric kernel, and write $\sigma_K^2 = \int t^2 K(t)\, dt$ (equal to 1
for the Gaussian):
\[
  \operatorname{bias}(x) = \E[\hat p(x)] - p(x) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\, h^2 \sigma_K^2\, p''(x) .
\]
At a peak $p'' < 0$ and the estimate is too low; in a valley $p'' > 0$ and it is too high. Peaks are flattened and
valleys filled in, by an amount growing like $h^2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:kdebiasloo}, left: the peak of height $0.54$ is
reduced on average to $0.44$ with $h = 0.25$ and to $0.29$ with $h = 0.6$).

\paragraph{Size of the variance} Since $\hat p$ is an average of $n$ independent terms,
$\Var[\hat p(x)] = \Var(Z_1)/n \le \E[Z_1^2]/n$, and with the same substitution
\[
  \E[Z_1^2] = \int K_h(x - u)^2 p(u)\, du = \frac{1}{h} \int K(t)^2 p(x - ht)\, dt \approx \frac{p(x)\, R(K)}{h},
  \qquad R(K) = \int K(t)^2\, dt .
\]
The inequality drops $(\E Z_1)^2/n \approx p(x)^2/n$, which is smaller than $p(x)R(K)/(nh)$ by a factor of order
$h$. Hence $\Var[\hat p(x)] \approx p(x) R(K)/(nh)$: few points fall under a narrow kernel, and their number
fluctuates.

\paragraph{The best bandwidth} The mean squared error at $x$ is bias squared plus variance (Note~4, Section~6),
\[
  \mathrm{MSE}(h) \approx \tfrac{1}{4} \sigma_K^4\, p''(x)^2\, h^4 + \frac{p(x)\, R(K)}{n h} = A h^4 + \frac{B}{nh}.
\]
A simple derivative finds the minimum:
\[
  \frac{d\, \mathrm{MSE}}{dh} = 4 A h^3 - \frac{B}{n h^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  h^* = \left( \frac{B}{4 A n} \right)^{1/5} \propto n^{-1/5},
\]
and inserting $h^*$ gives $\mathrm{MSE}(h^*) \propto n^{-4/5}$, because both terms scale alike:
$A h^{*4} \propto n^{-4/5}$ and $B/(n h^*) \propto n^{-1} \cdot n^{1/5} = n^{-4/5}$. The bandwidth must shrink as $n$
grows, to remove the bias, but slowly, so that $nh \to \infty$ and the variance vanishes too. The rate $n^{-4/5}$ is
faster than the $n^{-2/3}$ of histograms in one dimension (Note~3, Section~1). Integrating over $x$ and assuming that
$p$ is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma_p$ (the spread of the \emph{data}, not a kernel width) gives, for a
Gaussian kernel, $h^* = (4/3)^{1/5} \sigma_p\, n^{-1/5} \approx 1.06\,\sigma_p\, n^{-1/5}$, the
\emph{normal-reference rule} of Silverman~\cite{silverman1986}; in practice $\sigma_p$ is replaced by the sample
standard deviation $\hat\sigma_p$.

\paragraph{In $d$ dimensions} The bias is still of order $h^2$, but a kernel of width $h$ covers a volume $h^d$, so the
variance becomes $B/(nh^d)$. Repeating the derivative for $Ah^4 + B/(nh^d)$ gives $h^* \propto n^{-1/(d+4)}$ and
$\mathrm{MSE} \propto n^{-4/(d+4)}$. Ignoring constants, the accuracy that $n = 100$ points give in one dimension
($100^{-4/5} \approx 0.025$) requires in $d = 10$ that $n^{-4/14} = 100^{-4/5}$, i.e.\
$n = 100^{(4/5)(14/4)} = 100^{2.8} \approx 4 \times 10^5$ points. Kernels soften the
curse of dimensionality without lifting it.

\subsection{Choosing the bandwidth from the data}\label{sec:bandwidth}

The normal-reference rule is a quick starting point but assumes a single Gaussian bump; for the two-peaked density
of Figure~\ref{fig:kdebiasloo} it gives $h \approx 0.58$, almost twice the best value, and blurs the peaks. A data-driven
choice uses the principle of maximum likelihood (Note~2, Section~2), with care.

\paragraph{Why the plain likelihood fails} Maximising the log-likelihood of the training points themselves,
$\sum_i \log \hat p(x_i)$, does not work. The estimate at $x_i$ contains the term of $x_i$ itself, so in one dimension
\[
  \hat p(x_i) = \frac{1}{nh} \sum_{j} \varphi\!\left(\frac{x_i - x_j}{h}\right) \ge \frac{\varphi(0)}{nh},
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n \log \hat p(x_i) \ge n \log \frac{\varphi(0)}{nh} \xrightarrow[h \to 0]{} +\infty .
\]
The likelihood is maximised by a spike on every training point, the extreme of overfitting (Figure~\ref{fig:kdebiasloo},
right, grey curve).

\paragraph{Leave-one-out likelihood} Evaluate each point with the estimate built from the \emph{other} $n-1$ points,
\[
  \hat p_{-i}(\x_i) = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j \ne i} K_h(\x_i - \x_j), \qquad
  L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(h) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \hat p_{-i}(\x_i),
\]
and choose $h^* = \argmax_h L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(h)$. This is leave-one-out cross-validation (Note~4, Section~4) with the
negative log-likelihood as the loss. Now a tiny $h$ is punished: $\hat p_{-i}(\x_i)$ is nearly zero unless another
point sits right next to $\x_i$, and its logarithm tends to $-\infty$. A huge $h$ is punished too, because it spreads
the probability thinly everywhere. The maximum lies in between. (If two data points coincide, as with rounded
measurements, $L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(h) \to +\infty$ as $h \to 0$ again: remove duplicates, add jitter below the
measurement resolution, or restrict $h$ from below.)

\begin{worked}{leave-one-out bandwidth for four points}
Data $0, 1, 3, 4$ as before. With $h = 1$, leaving out $x_1 = 0$, the other points are at distances $1, 3, 4$:
\[
  \hat p_{-1}(0) = \tfrac{1}{3}\big[\varphi(1) + \varphi(3) + \varphi(4)\big] = \tfrac{1}{3}(0.2420 + 0.0044 + 0.0001)
  = 0.0822 ,
\]
and leaving out $x_2 = 1$ (distances $1, 2, 3$) gives $\tfrac{1}{3}(0.2420 + 0.0540 + 0.0044) = 0.1001$. By symmetry,
$x_4$ behaves like $x_1$ and $x_3$ like $x_2$, so $L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(1) = 2\log 0.0822 + 2\log 0.1001 = -9.60$. The
same computation for other bandwidths (each term now divided by $h$):

\begin{tabular}{@{}ccccc@{}}
\toprule
$h$ & $\hat p_{-1}(0) = \hat p_{-4}(4)$ & $\hat p_{-2}(1) = \hat p_{-3}(3)$ & $L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(h)$ &
$\sum_i \log \hat p(x_i)$, self included \\
\midrule
0.5 & 0.0360 & 0.0361 & $-13.29$ & $-5.94$ \\

\end{center}
Leave-one-out picks $h = 2$ among the candidates (a fine grid gives $h^* \approx 1.97$), while the self-included
likelihood keeps improving as $h$ decreases, as the bound above predicts. The normal-reference rule, with the sample
standard deviation $\hat\sigma_p = 1.83$, gives $1.06 \times 1.83 \times 4^{-1/5} = 1.47$, the same order.
\end{worked}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Bandwidth selection by leave-one-out log-likelihood}
\label{alg:loo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $\x_1, \dots, \x_n \in \R^d$; candidate bandwidths $h_1, \dots, h_H$
\State $D_{ij} \gets \norm{\x_i - \x_j}^2$ for all $i, j$ \Comment{once, $O(n^2 d)$}
\For{$\ell = 1, \dots, H$}
  \State $L_\ell \gets 0$
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $a_j \gets -D_{ij}/(2h_\ell^2) - \frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi h_\ell^2)$ for all $j \ne i$ \Comment{$\log K_{h}(\x_i - \x_j)$}
    \State $a^* \gets \max_{j \ne i} a_j$
    \State $L_\ell \gets L_\ell + a^* + \log \sum_{j \ne i} \exp(a_j - a^*) - \log(n-1)$ \Comment{$\log \hat p_{-i}(\x_i)$}
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $h_{\ell^*}$ with $\ell^* = \argmax_\ell L_\ell$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{leave-one-out bandwidth selection (Algorithm~\ref{alg:loo})}
$O(n^2 d)$ for the pairwise distances, then $O(n^2)$ per candidate bandwidth: $O(n^2 d + Hn^2)$ in total. Storing
$D$ costs $O(n^2)$ memory --- 800\,MB in double precision for $n = 10^4$ --- so for larger $n$ the rows are recomputed
for every candidate ($O(Hn^2 d)$ time, $O(nd)$ memory), or the likelihood is evaluated on a random subset of points.
Since $L_{\mathrm{LOO}}(h)$ is usually smooth with a single maximum, a coarse logarithmic grid of 10--20 values of $h$
followed by a refinement suffices. All candidate bandwidths reuse the same matrix $D$, and each is a batch of
element-wise operations on it, which vectorises well on a GPU.
\end{runtime}

\paragraph{From densities to classifiers} Estimating one density per class with a KDE and combining the
estimates with class priors through Bayes' rule gives a classifier (Note~6, Section~2). With $n_c$ points in class
$c$, one common bandwidth $h = \sigma$ and priors equal to the class frequencies $n_c/n$,
\[
  \hat P(c \mid \x) \propto \frac{n_c}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n_c} \sum_{i : y_i = c} K_h(\x - \x_i)
  = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i : y_i = c} K_h(\x - \x_i) ,
\]
and after normalising over $c$ the constants $1/n$ and $(2\pi h^2)^{-d/2}$ cancel: the result is the score $s_c(\x)$
of the Gaussian Parzen classifier of Section~\ref{sec:soft}.

\begin{physics}{unbinned shapes}
When a particle-physics analysis needs the distribution of an observable for signal or background, but has only a
finite set of simulated events, kernel estimation provides a smooth, unbinned template; adaptive kernels whose width
grows in the tails, where events are scarce, are widely used~\cite{cranmer2001}. The adaptive width answers
exactly the trade-off above: the relative error of the estimate, $\sqrt{\Var[\hat p(x)]}/p(x) \approx
\sqrt{R(K)/(nh\,p(x))}$, is largest where $p$ is small, and a wider kernel there brings it down.
\end{physics}

\section{Distances}\label{sec:distances}

Every method in this note is only as good as its notion of ``near''. A \emph{metric} on a set satisfies, for all
$\vect{a}, \vect{b}, \vect{c}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item non-negativity: $d(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) \ge 0$, with $d(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) = 0$ if and only if $\vect{a} = \vect{b}$;
\item symmetry: $d(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) = d(\vect{b}, \vect{a})$;
\item triangle inequality: $d(\vect{a}, \vect{c}) \le d(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) + d(\vect{b}, \vect{c})$.
\end{enumerate}
The triangle inequality is what makes \emph{metric} search structures (ball trees, vantage-point trees) work: it lets
an algorithm conclude that a point is far from the query without computing the distance, from the distances to a
third point. The $k$-d tree of Section~\ref{sec:kdtree} needs less: only the fact that one coordinate difference never
exceeds the Euclidean distance.

\begin{minipage}[c]{0.6\linewidth}
\paragraph{Minkowski distances} $d_p(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) = \big(\sum_j |a_j - b_j|^p\big)^{1/p}$ for $p \ge 1$. The
cases $p = 1$ (Manhattan), $p = 2$ (Euclidean) and $p \to \infty$ ($\max_j |a_j - b_j|$) are the common ones. Their
unit balls (Figure~\ref{fig:balls}) show what each considers ``equally far''. Always
$d_\infty \le d_2 \le d_1$: the larger $p$, the more the distance is dominated by the largest coordinate difference.
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.36\linewidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/unit_balls.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{Unit balls $\{\vect{a} : d_p(\vect{a}, \vect{0}) = 1\}$ in $\R^2$.}
\label{fig:balls}
\end{minipage}

\paragraph{When $p < 1$} The formula still measures dissimilarity, but it is no longer a metric. Take
$\vect{a} = (0,0)$, $\vect{b} = (1,0)$, $\vect{c} = (1,1)$. Then $d_p(\vect{a}, \vect{b}) = d_p(\vect{b}, \vect{c}) = 1$
for every $p$, while
\[
  d_p(\vect{a}, \vect{c}) = (1^p + 1^p)^{1/p} = 2^{1/p} .
\]
The triangle inequality requires $2^{1/p} \le 2$, i.e.\ $1/p \le 1$, i.e.\ $p \ge 1$. For $p = 1/2$ the detour through
$\vect{b}$ has length 2 while the direct route has length $2^2 = 4$. Geometrically, the ``unit ball'' for $p < 1$
(dotted in Figure~\ref{fig:balls}) is not convex: it is pinched towards the axes.

\paragraph{Similarities for other data} For embeddings and documents, the cosine similarity of Note~1, Section~6,
compares directions; as a neighbourhood measure one uses the \emph{cosine distance} $1 - \cos$, which is not a metric
(it vanishes for $\vect{b} = 2\vect{a}$ and can violate the triangle inequality). For binary fingerprints, such as
the substructure bit vectors used to search chemical databases, the Tanimoto (Jaccard) similarity
$|\vect{a} \wedge \vect{b}| / |\vect{a} \vee \vect{b}|$ counts shared bits relative to bits set in either vector: for
$\vect{a} = 11010$ and $\vect{b} = 10110$ two bits are shared and four are set in either, giving $2/4 = 0.5$. One minus
the Tanimoto similarity is a metric on bit vectors.

\needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{worked}{four distances between the same two vectors}
Let $\vect{a} = (1, 2, 2)$ and $\vect{b} = (4, 0, 3)$, so $\vect{a} - \vect{b} = (-3, 2, -1)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $L_1$: $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$.
\item $L_2$: $\sqrt{9 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74$.
\item $L_\infty$: $\max(3, 2, 1) = 3$.
\item Cosine: $\norm{\vect{a}} = 3$, $\norm{\vect{b}} = 5$, $\vect{a}\T\vect{b} = 4 + 0 + 6 = 10$, so the cosine
similarity $\vect{a}\T\vect{b}/(\norm{\vect{a}}\,\norm{\vect{b}})$ is $10/15 = 2/3$ and the cosine distance
$1 - 2/3 = 1/3$.
\end{itemize}
The ordering $3 \le 3.74 \le 6$ illustrates $d_\infty \le d_2 \le d_1$. Replacing $\vect{b}$ by $10\vect{b}$
leaves the cosine similarity at $2/3$ but changes all three Minkowski distances (the Euclidean one becomes
$\sqrt{2309} \approx 48.1$): cosine compares directions only.
\end{worked}

\paragraph{When Euclidean distance misleads} Three problems are common, each with a standard remedy.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Units and scales.} A feature measured in GeV and another in radians contribute to the distance in
proportions set by the units. Standardise each feature, $z_j = (x_j - \hat\mu_j)/\hat\sigma_j$, with the mean
$\hat\mu_j$ and standard deviation $\hat\sigma_j$ computed on the training set only and then applied unchanged to
validation, test and future data (Note~1, Section~5; Algorithm~\ref{alg:standardise}).
\item \emph{Correlations and irrelevant features.} Correlated features count the same information twice --- a
duplicated feature doubles its weight in $\norm{\cdot}^2$ --- and irrelevant ones add noise to every distance. The
Mahalanobis distance of Note~2, Section~4, corrects for correlations; feature selection or a learned projection
removes irrelevant directions.
\item \emph{Invariances.} Two calorimeter images of the same narrow jet, one displaced by a single cell, can be
farther apart in pixel space than images of two different jets: the energy deposits no longer overlap. Invariant
features (Note~1, Section~3) or a distance that ignores the transformation are needed; Section~\ref{sec:tangent}
constructs such a distance.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Feature standardisation: fit on the training set, apply everywhere (an instance of the \textsc{Fit}/\textsc{Apply} step of Note~1, Section~5)}
\label{alg:standardise}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Fit}{$\x_1, \dots, \x_n$} \Comment{training inputs only}
  \For{$j = 1, \dots, d$}
    \State $\hat\mu_j \gets \frac{1}{n} \sum_i x_{ij}$; \ $\hat\sigma_j \gets \big(\frac{1}{n} \sum_i (x_{ij} - \hat\mu_j)^2\big)^{1/2}$
    \State \textbf{if} $\hat\sigma_j = 0$ \textbf{then} $\hat\sigma_j \gets 1$ \Comment{constant feature: avoid $0/0$}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\hat{\vmu}, \hat{\vect{\sigma}}$
\EndFunction
\Function{Apply}{$\x$; $\hat{\vmu}, \hat{\vect{\sigma}}$} \Comment{training, validation, test and new inputs alike}
  \State \Return $\vect{z}$ with $z_j = (x_j - \hat\mu_j)/\hat\sigma_j$, $j = 1, \dots, d$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{standardisation (Algorithm~\ref{alg:standardise})}
\emph{Fit}: two passes over the training data, $O(nd)$ time. \emph{Apply}: $O(d)$ per vector (one subtraction and
one division per feature). \emph{Memory}: $O(d)$ for $\hat{\vmu}$ and $\hat{\vect{\sigma}}$. Negligible next to
any $k$-NN query, which is why there is no excuse to skip it.
\end{runtime}

\needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{worked}{the nearest neighbour depends on the units}
Two features: an energy $E$ and an angle $\theta$. Query $\x = (50\ \mathrm{GeV}, 0\ \mathrm{rad})$, candidates
$\x_A = (52\ \mathrm{GeV}, 1.5\ \mathrm{rad})$ and $\x_B = (47\ \mathrm{GeV}, 0.1\ \mathrm{rad})$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Energy in GeV: $d(\x, \x_A) = \sqrt{2^2 + 1.5^2} = 2.5$ and $d(\x, \x_B) = \sqrt{3^2 + 0.1^2} = 3.002$.
Nearest: A.
\item Energy in TeV: $d(\x, \x_A) = \sqrt{0.002^2 + 1.5^2} \approx 1.500$ and
$d(\x, \x_B) = \sqrt{0.003^2 + 0.1^2} \approx 0.100$. Nearest: B.
\item Standardised, with training-set standard deviations $10\ \mathrm{GeV}$ and $1\ \mathrm{rad}$: the differences
become $(0.2, 1.5)$ and $(-0.3, 0.1)$, so $d = 1.513$ and $0.316$. Nearest: B.
\end{itemize}
A change of units, which carries no physical information, changed the answer. Standardisation makes the result
independent of units; whether it weights the features \emph{correctly} is a separate question, answered by validation
or by learning the metric.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Invariances and tangent distance}\label{sec:tangent}

\paragraph{Patterns live on low-dimensional manifolds} Let a pattern $\vect{a} \in \R^d$ (an image, a signal) undergo a
transformation with $r$ continuous parameters $\vect{\alpha} \in \R^r$ --- a shift across the detector, a rotation, a
change of scale or of line thickness --- written $F(\vect{a}; \vect{\alpha})$, with $F(\vect{a}; \vect{0}) =
\vect{a}$. As $\vect{\alpha}$ varies, the transformed patterns trace an $r$-dimensional curved surface, a
\emph{manifold}, inside the $d$-dimensional pixel space. That real data lie near such low-dimensional sets is the
reason learning in high dimension is possible at all (Note~3, Section~4). It is also the reason Euclidean distance
fails: it measures the straight chord between two points of $\R^d$ and ignores whether one can be moved into the other
along its manifold. The distance we want is between the \emph{manifolds} of two patterns, $\min_{\vect{\alpha},
\vect{\beta}} \norm{F(\vect{a}; \vect{\alpha}) - F(\vect{b}; \vect{\beta})}$, but that is a non-linear minimisation
for every pair of points.

\paragraph{Tangent distance} Simard, LeCun and Denker~\cite{simard1993} replace each manifold by its tangent plane at
the pattern. A first-order Taylor expansion in $\vect{\alpha}$ gives
\[
  F(\vect{a}; \vect{\alpha}) \approx \vect{a} + T\vect{\alpha}, \qquad
  T = \big[\vect{t}_1, \dots, \vect{t}_r\big] \in \R^{d \times r}, \qquad
  \vect{t}_\ell = \frac{\partial F(\vect{a}; \vect{\alpha})}{\partial \alpha_\ell}\bigg|_{\vect{\alpha} = \vect{0}} ,
\]
where each \emph{tangent vector} $\vect{t}_\ell$ is the change of the pattern per unit of the $\ell$-th parameter. In
practice it is a finite difference, $\vect{t}_\ell \approx (F(\vect{a}; \varepsilon \vect{e}_\ell) - \vect{a})/\varepsilon$;
for a shift of a sampled signal by $s$ pixels to the right, $a_j(s) = a_{j - s}$, the central difference gives
$t_j = (a_{j-1} - a_{j+1})/2$ (zero outside the signal). The \emph{one-sided tangent distance} from $\vect{a}$ to
$\vect{x}$ is
\[
  d_{\mathrm{tan}}(\vect{a}, \x) = \min_{\vect{\alpha} \in \R^r} \norm{\vect{a} + T\vect{\alpha} - \x} .
\]
The vector $\vect{a} + T\vect{\alpha}$ is linear in $\vect{\alpha}$, so this is a least-squares problem with $r$
unknowns (Note~8, Section~2): setting the gradient $2T\T(\vect{a} + T\vect{\alpha} - \x)$ to zero gives the normal
equations
\[
  (T\T T)\, \vect{\alpha}^* = T\T (\x - \vect{a}), \qquad\text{and for one tangent vector}\qquad
  \alpha^* = \frac{\vect{t}\T(\x - \vect{a})}{\vect{t}\T\vect{t}} .
\]
The \emph{two-sided} version also moves $\x$ along its own tangent plane, $\min_{\vect{\alpha}, \vect{\beta}}
\norm{\vect{a} + T_{\vect{a}}\vect{\alpha} - \x - T_{\x}\vect{\beta}}$, with $2r$ unknowns; it is symmetric in its
two arguments, which the one-sided version is not. Neither is a metric in the sense of the three axioms above (two
different patterns can be at distance zero, and the triangle inequality can fail), and both are trustworthy only for \emph{small} transformations, where the plane is
close to the manifold; images are often blurred slightly first so that the finite differences are meaningful.

\begin{worked}{tangent distance for a shifted signal}
Two training signals of length 5: a ``wide'' pulse $\vect{a} = (0, 1, 2, 1, 0)$ and a ``narrow'' spike
$\vect{z} = (0, 0, 0, 2, 0)$. The query $\x = (0, 0, 1, 2, 1)$ is the wide pulse moved one sample to the right.
\begin{itemize}
\item Euclidean: $\x - \vect{a} = (0, -1, -1, 1, 1)$, so $\norm{\x - \vect{a}} = \sqrt{4} = 2$; $\x - \vect{z} =
(0, 0, 1, 0, 1)$, so $\norm{\x - \vect{z}} = \sqrt{2} = 1.41$. 1-NN says ``narrow'': wrong.
\item Tangent vector of $\vect{a}$ for a shift, $t_j = (a_{j-1} - a_{j+1})/2$: $\vect{t} = (-0.5, -1, 0, 1, 0.5)$.
Then $\vect{t}\T\vect{t} = 2.5$ and $\vect{t}\T(\x - \vect{a}) = 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0.5 = 2.5$, so $\alpha^* = 1$: the
best move is one sample to the right, as it should be. The residual is $\vect{a} + \vect{t} - \x =
(-0.5, 0, 1, 0, -0.5)$, so $d_{\mathrm{tan}}(\vect{a}, \x) = \sqrt{1.5} = 1.22$.
\item Tangent vector of $\vect{z}$: $(0, 0, -1, 0, 1)$, orthogonal to $\x - \vect{z}$, so $\alpha^* = 0$ and
$d_{\mathrm{tan}}(\vect{z}, \x) = 1.41$: shifting the spike does not help.
\end{itemize}
With tangent distance the query is nearer the wide pulse ($1.22 < 1.41$), and 1-NN is right. The residual is not zero
although $\x$ \emph{is} an exact shift of $\vect{a}$: a one-sample shift is not small for this short signal, and the
tangent plane is only a first-order approximation of the manifold.
\end{worked}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Nearest neighbour with one-sided tangent distance}
\label{alg:tangent}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training points $\x_1, \dots, \x_n \in \R^d$, each with its tangent matrix $T_i \in \R^{d \times r}$
\Function{Prepare}{} \Comment{once, after the tangent vectors are computed}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $G_i \gets T_i\T T_i$ and its Cholesky factor \Comment{$O(dr^2 + r^3)$; add $\epsilon I$ if singular}
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\Function{TanDist}{$i, \x$}
  \State $\vect{g} \gets T_i\T(\x - \x_i)$ \Comment{$O(dr)$}
  \State solve $G_i \vect{\alpha} = \vect{g}$ with the stored factor \Comment{$O(r^2)$}
  \State \Return $\norm{\x_i + T_i\vect{\alpha} - \x}$ \Comment{$O(dr)$}
\EndFunction
\Function{NearestTan}{$\x$}
  \State \Return $\argmin_i \Call{TanDist}{i, \x}$ \Comment{or the $k$ smallest, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn}}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{tangent distance (Algorithm~\ref{alg:tangent})}
Notation: $r$ transformations (tangent vectors per point). \emph{Training}: computing the tangent vectors needs $r$
transformed copies of each point, $O(ndr)$ if a transformation costs $O(d)$; \textsc{Prepare} adds
$O(n(dr^2 + r^3))$. \emph{Memory}: $O(ndr)$ for the tangents (with the data, $r + 1$ times the raw data) plus $O(nr^2)$ for the
factors. \emph{Prediction per query}: $O(n(dr + r^2))$, about $r$ times a Euclidean query. The two-sided version
depends on the query's own tangents, so its $2r \times 2r$ system must be formed and solved for every pair:
$O(n(dr^2 + r^3))$ per query. A common speed-up is to rank all points by the cheap Euclidean distance first and to
compute the tangent distance only for the few hundred nearest.
\end{runtime}

\begin{modern}{learning the metric}
Today the distance is usually learned. Contrastive methods train a network so that two transformed views of the same
image map to nearby embeddings and views of different images map far apart~\cite{chen2020}; image--text models do the
same for an image and its caption~\cite{radford2021}. The invariances that tangent distance builds in by hand are
learned from the transformations used in training, and the plain cosine or Euclidean distance in the learned space
then works well for $k$-NN, retrieval and clustering.
\end{modern}

\begin{physics}{a metric for collider events}
Komiske, Metodiev and Thaler defined the distance between two collider events as the minimal ``work'' needed to
rearrange one event's energy flow into the other's --- an optimal-transport, or earth mover's, distance --- which
respects the physical requirement that soft or collinear emissions should change it only slightly~\cite{komiske2019}.
With this metric, even a simple $k$-NN classifier separates jet types well, and geometric quantities of the space of events
become measurable. Choosing the distance is where the physics enters.
\end{physics}

\begin{keyidea}[Summary]
$k$-NN and Parzen windows predict by a weighted vote of training examples, with weights given by neighbourhood
membership or by a kernel; the same formula --- the weighted mean that minimises a weighted squared error --- serves
regression and classification, and its denominator, the total weight near the query, normalised by $n$ and the window
volume, is the kernel density estimate. They need no training, are consistent as
$n \to \infty$ provided the neighbourhood shrinks slowly enough ($k \to \infty$ with $k/n \to 0$, or $h \to 0$ with
$nh^d \to \infty$), whereas 1-NN only reaches at most twice the Bayes error, and they have one main hyperparameter --- $k$, the radius or the bandwidth --- that trades bias for
variance; for KDE the bias is $K_h * p - p \approx \tfrac{1}{2}h^2 \sigma_K^2 p''$ and the variance
$\approx p R(K)/(nh)$, giving $h^* \propto n^{-1/5}$, and leave-one-out (never the plain training likelihood) selects
$k$ or $h$ from data. Prediction costs $O(nd)$ per query unless a tree (low $d$) or an approximate index (high $d$)
is used, and the log-sum-exp trick keeps kernel weights finite. Their weak point is the distance: it must reflect the
problem's units, correlations and invariances (standardisation, Mahalanobis distance, tangent distance), which is
why modern systems learn the representation and then use
simple neighbourhood methods on top of it.
\end{keyidea}
