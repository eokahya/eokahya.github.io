\NoteTitle{7}{4}{Optimisation: gradients, gradient\\ descent and its stochastic variants}{Optimisation}{%
Training a model means minimising an objective over its parameters. This note reviews the calculus needed --- partial
derivatives, gradients, the Hessian --- and develops gradient descent: the role of the learning rate and of
conditioning, convex and non-convex landscapes, and the mini-batch stochastic gradients that make training on large
datasets possible. Momentum, Newton's method and Adam complete the toolbox used to train every neural network,
including the large language models whose training recipes close the note.}

\section{Learning is optimisation}\label{sec:problem}

The training phase of most algorithms in this course solves
\[
  \theta^* = \argmin_{\theta \in \R^p} J(\theta), \qquad
  J(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n L\big(f_\theta(\x_i), y_i\big) + \lambda\,\Omega(\theta),
\]
the regularised empirical risk of Note~4, Section~7, as a function of the $p$ parameters. Minimising the sum or the
mean is the same problem; the mean keeps the scale independent of $n$. Picture $J$ as a landscape over parameter
space: training means finding a low point.

\section{Derivatives, gradients and curvature}\label{sec:calculus}

For $J : \R^p \to \R$, the partial derivative $\partial J / \partial\theta_k$ measures the sensitivity of $J$ to a
change of $\theta_k$ alone, the others held fixed. The \emph{gradient} collects them,
\[
  \nabla J(\theta) = \Big( \frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_1}(\theta), \dots, \frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_p}(\theta) \Big)\T \in \R^p ,
\]
and the matrix of second derivatives is the \emph{Hessian}, $H_{kl} = \partial^2 J / \partial\theta_k\partial\theta_l$.
Taylor's theorem summarises what they mean:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taylor}
  J(\theta + \vect{\delta}) = J(\theta) + \nabla J(\theta)\T\vect{\delta} + \tfrac12 \vect{\delta}\T H(\theta)\,\vect{\delta} + O(\norm{\vect{\delta}}^3) .
\end{equation}
Among all small steps of fixed length, the first-order term is most negative for $\vect{\delta}$ pointing along
$-\nabla J$: the gradient points uphill, in the direction of steepest ascent, and is perpendicular to the contour
lines of $J$. At a minimum the slope vanishes, $\nabla J(\theta^*) = \vect{0}$. A point with zero gradient is a local
minimum if $H$ is positive definite, a local maximum if it is negative definite and a \emph{saddle point} if $H$ has
eigenvalues of both signs.

\paragraph{Closed form or search} Sometimes $\nabla J = \vect{0}$ can be solved analytically: the maximum-likelihood
Gaussian (Note~2, Section~6) and linear and ridge regression (Note~8) are examples. Usually it cannot, and we must
search. Trying all values on a grid is hopeless beyond a handful of parameters --- ten values per parameter and
$p = 20$ already give $10^{20}$ evaluations, the curse of dimensionality of Note~3 once more --- so we follow the
local slope instead.

\section{Gradient descent}\label{sec:gd}

Starting from an initial $\theta_0$ (random, or by a heuristic suited to the model), repeat
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gd}
  \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \nabla J(\theta_t),
\end{equation}
where the \emph{learning rate} (step size) $\eta > 0$ is a hyperparameter. Stop when the updates no longer change
$\theta$ appreciably, for instance when $\norm{\nabla J(\theta_t)} < \epsilon$, or earlier when the validation error
stops improving (early stopping, Note~4, Section~7).

\paragraph{What the learning rate does} Near a minimum, \eqref{eq:taylor} says $J$ is approximately quadratic,
$J(\theta) \approx J^* + \frac12 (\theta - \theta^*)\T A (\theta - \theta^*)$ with $A$ the Hessian at $\theta^*$.
Then \eqref{eq:gd} becomes $\theta_{t+1} - \theta^* = (I - \eta A)(\theta_t - \theta^*)$, and along each eigenvector of
$A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ the error is multiplied by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$ at every step. Three consequences
follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item Gradient descent converges only if $|1 - \eta\lambda_i| < 1$ for every $i$, i.e.\ $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$; a
larger step makes the steepest direction oscillate with growing amplitude.
\item The flattest direction shrinks by $1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}$ per step, slow if $\lambda_{\min} \ll \lambda_{\max}$.
\item With the best fixed step, the error decreases by a factor $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step, where
$\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the \emph{condition number}. Elongated valleys are hard.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:gdpaths} shows a valley with $\kappa = 25$. With $\eta = 0.01$ the descent is stable but crawls; with
$\eta = 0.075$, close to the limit $2/25 = 0.08$, it zigzags across the valley and makes steady progress. This is why
features are standardised before training (Note~5, Section~5): very different feature scales stretch the valley.

\paragraph{Schedules} A fixed step is rarely ideal: large steps make fast early progress, small steps settle into
the minimum. A classical schedule is $\eta_t = \eta_0\, t_0 / (t_0 + t)$, with $\eta_0$ and $t_0$ hyperparameters.
Modern deep-learning practice uses a short linear \emph{warm-up} from a small value, which avoids instabilities while
the parameters are still random, followed by a slow decay, often along a half cosine.

\section{Convex and non-convex objectives}\label{sec:convex}

A function is \emph{convex} if the chord between any two points of its graph lies above the graph; for twice
differentiable $J$, if $H$ is positive semi-definite everywhere. Every local minimum of a convex function is global,
so gradient descent with a suitable step reaches the global minimum from any start. Least squares, ridge regression,
logistic regression and the linear support vector machine (Notes~8 and~9) all have convex objectives.

Neural networks do not. Their landscapes have many stationary points, and where gradient descent ends depends on where
it starts (Figure~\ref{fig:nonconvex}). In high dimension the picture is less bleak than this one-dimensional cartoon
suggests: most stationary points with high loss are saddle points rather than poor local minima, because it is unlikely
that all $p$ curvatures are positive at once, and the minima that training finds tend to have similar loss~\cite{dauphin2014}.
Slow progress near saddles, rather than being trapped, is the more common difficulty.

\section{Stochastic gradients}\label{sec:sgd}

The gradient of a mean is the mean of the gradients:
\[
  \nabla J(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla_\theta L\big(f_\theta(\x_i), y_i\big) \quad (+\,\lambda\nabla\Omega).
\]
\emph{Batch} gradient descent computes all $n$ terms before every update, which costs a full pass over the data per
step. \emph{Mini-batch} gradient descent replaces the mean over $\Dtrain$ by the mean over a random subset $B$ of
$n' \ll n$ examples:
\[
  \vect{g}_B(\theta) = \frac{1}{n'} \sum_{i \in B} \nabla_\theta L\big(f_\theta(\x_i), y_i\big), \qquad
  \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \vect{g}_B(\theta) .
\]
The case $n' = 1$ is \emph{stochastic} (online) gradient descent, SGD; $n' = n$ recovers the batch method. If $B$ is
drawn at random, $\vect{g}_B$ is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla J$ whose variance falls like $1/n'$. In practice the
data are shuffled once per \emph{epoch} (one pass over $\Dtrain$) and cut into consecutive mini-batches.

Figure~\ref{fig:sgd} compares the three on logistic regression with $n = 2000$. After one epoch, batch gradient descent
has taken one step; mini-batch descent with $n' = 32$ has taken 63 and is already within $0.013$ of the optimal loss.
With a constant step, however, the stochastic methods do not converge: they fluctuate in a band whose width is set by
the gradient noise and the learning rate. Pure SGD, the noisiest, ends further from the optimum after 20 epochs than
batch descent. Mini-batches are the compromise used in practice, and they are also what parallel hardware wants: the
$n'$ gradients are computed together as matrix products on a GPU, so a mini-batch of 256 costs little more time than
a single example. Decreasing the learning rate removes the noise floor at the end of training.

\section{Beyond plain gradient descent}\label{sec:beyond}

\paragraph{Momentum} Accumulate a velocity and move with it,
$\vect{v}_{t+1} = \beta\,\vect{v}_t - \eta\nabla J(\theta_t)$, $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \vect{v}_{t+1}$, with
$0 \le \beta < 1$. Components of the gradient that keep their sign add up; those that alternate, as across a valley,
cancel. With well-chosen $\eta$ and $\beta$ the convergence factor on a quadratic improves from
$(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ to $(\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)$~\cite{polyak1964}: in Figure~\ref{fig:gdpaths}
momentum reaches $J \approx 10^{-9}$ in the same forty steps.

\paragraph{Newton's method} Minimise the quadratic model \eqref{eq:taylor} exactly:
$\theta \leftarrow \theta - H^{-1}\nabla J$. On a quadratic objective it converges in one step whatever the condition
number, and near a minimum it converges quadratically. But forming and solving with the $p \times p$ Hessian costs
$O(p^2)$ memory and $O(p^3)$ time, it is unreliable far from a minimum or near saddles, and it does not combine well with
noisy mini-batch gradients. Quasi-Newton methods such as L-BFGS build a low-rank curvature estimate from successive
gradients and are excellent for medium-sized, deterministic problems; logistic regression is often trained with
Newton's method itself.

\paragraph{Adaptive methods} Adam keeps exponential moving averages of the gradient and of its element-wise square, and
divides the first by the square root of the second~\cite{kingma2015}. Each parameter thus gets its own step size,
which compensates for very different gradient scales across a network. AdamW applies weight decay directly to the
parameters instead of through the gradient, which matters for adaptive methods~\cite{loshchilov2019}; it is the
default optimiser for transformers.

\paragraph{Where the gradients come from} For a neural network with millions of parameters, $\nabla J$ is computed by
reverse-mode \emph{automatic differentiation} --- backpropagation --- at a cost of a small constant times one evaluation
of $J$, exactly, not by finite differences~\cite{baydin2018}. This is the subject of the rest of week~4.

\paragraph{Constraints} When parameters must satisfy constraints (non-negative, summing to one, inside a ball), one can
project onto the feasible set after each step, reparameterise (a softmax always sums to one) or use Lagrange
multipliers. Linear and quadratic programmes form a well-understood class; the support vector machine of Note~9 is a
quadratic programme.

\begin{physics}{optimisers as mechanics}
Gradient descent with small steps follows the \emph{gradient flow} $\dot\theta = -\nabla J(\theta)$: overdamped motion
of a particle in the potential $J$. Momentum restores inertia --- Polyak's ``heavy ball'', $m\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta
= -\nabla J$, discretised --- which is why it rolls through narrow valleys instead of zigzagging. The noise of
mini-batch gradients acts like a temperature: SGD resembles Langevin dynamics, and adding properly scaled Gaussian
noise to the updates turns it into a sampler of the posterior distribution of the parameters~\cite{welling2011}.
\end{physics}

\begin{modern}{how a large language model is trained}
The recipe for GPT-3, typical of large models, uses every idea above~\cite{brown2020}: Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and
$\beta_2 = 0.95$, the gradient norm clipped at 1.0 to tame rare large steps, a linear warm-up over the first 375 million
tokens followed by cosine decay of the learning rate, weight decay of 0.1, and mini-batches that grow to about
3.2 million tokens. The objective is the next-token cross-entropy (Note~2, Section~2), and each update is a mini-batch
gradient step of Section~\ref{sec:sgd}, on $1.75 \times 10^{11}$ parameters.
\end{modern}

\begin{keyidea}[Summary]
The gradient points uphill and vanishes at minima; the Hessian describes curvature. Gradient descent steps against the
gradient with learning rate $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and its speed is limited by the condition number. Convex
objectives have a single basin; neural networks do not, but saddles rather than bad minima are the usual obstacle.
Mini-batch stochastic gradients trade exactness for many cheap steps and leave a noise floor that a decaying learning
rate removes. Momentum, Newton and Adam reshape the steps; automatic differentiation supplies the gradients.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{exercises}
\item For $J(\theta) = \frac12 \theta\T A\theta - \vect{b}\T\theta$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite, find
$\nabla J$, $H$ and $\theta^*$. Show that gradient descent with $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min})$ reduces
the error by $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step, and that Newton's method converges in one step.
\item Rescale the second coordinate of the problem in Figure~\ref{fig:gdpaths} by a factor 5. What is the new condition
number, and what does this say about standardising features?
\item Show that the mini-batch gradient is unbiased and that its covariance is $\frac{1}{n'}$ times that of a single-example
gradient when the batch is drawn with replacement.
\item Implement batch, mini-batch and stochastic gradient descent for logistic regression and reproduce
Figure~\ref{fig:sgd}. Then use a decaying learning rate $\eta_t = \eta_0/(1 + t/t_0)$ for SGD and report the excess loss
after 20 epochs.
\item Find the stationary points of $J(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \theta_1^2 - \theta_2^2 + \frac12\theta_2^4$ and classify
them with the Hessian. Starting at $(1, 0)$, where does gradient descent go? And starting at $(1, 10^{-6})$?
\item Estimate the memory needed to store the Hessian of a model with $p = 10^8$ parameters in 32-bit floats, and compare
with storing the parameters and the two moment estimates of Adam.
\end{exercises}
