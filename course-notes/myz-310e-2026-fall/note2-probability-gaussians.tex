\NoteTitle{2}{1}{Probability distributions\\ and the Gaussian}{Probability and Gaussians}{%
Every method in this course either estimates a probability distribution or makes predictions that are best
understood through one. This note collects the probability we need --- how distributions are described, the four
things we do with them, and the multivariate Gaussian in detail --- and connects it to two pillars of modern
machine learning: maximum-likelihood training of language models and Gaussian noise in diffusion models. Notation
follows Note~1, Section~2.}

\section{Describing a distribution}\label{sec:describe}

A random variable $X$ is described by how its probability is spread over its possible values. Three descriptions
are in common use.

\paragraph{Cumulative distribution function} For a real-valued $X$, the c.d.f.\ is $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. It is
non-decreasing, tends to $0$ as $x \to -\infty$ and to $1$ as $x \to +\infty$, and it exists for every random
variable, discrete or continuous. For a vector $\X = (X_1, \dots, X_d)$ the inequality is read component-wise:
$F(\x) = P(X_1 \le x_1, \dots, X_d \le x_d)$.

\paragraph{Probability table (discrete variables)} If $X$ takes finitely or countably many values, its distribution
is the list of probabilities $P(X = x)$, which are non-negative and sum to one. For several discrete variables the
table becomes an array with one axis per variable; a joint table for $d$ variables with $m$ values each has $m^d$
entries --- a first sign of the difficulty Note~3 studies.

\paragraph{Density (continuous variables)} If $F$ is differentiable, the probability density function is
$p(x) = F'(x)$, and probabilities of regions are integrals,
\[
  P(\X \in A) = \int_A p(\x)\, d\x, \qquad \int_{\R^d} p(\x)\,d\x = 1 .
\]
A density is not a probability: it carries units of $1/[x]$ and may exceed one. A Gaussian of width $\sigma =
0.1$~mm has a peak density of about $4\ \mathrm{mm}^{-1}$.

\paragraph{The empirical distribution} Given a sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the empirical c.d.f.\
$\hat F(x) = \frac{1}{n}\#\{i : x_i \le x\}$ is a step function that jumps by $1/n$ at each data point. By the law
of large numbers $\hat F(x) \to F(x)$ for every $x$; the convergence is even uniform in $x$ (Glivenko--Cantelli).
The empirical distribution is the simplest estimate of a distribution, and every learning algorithm can be viewed
as a way of smoothing it.

\subsection{Joint, marginal and conditional}

For two variables with joint density $p(\x, \vect{z})$ (sums replace integrals in the discrete case):
\begin{align*}
  \text{marginal:} \quad & p(\x) = \int p(\x, \vect{z})\, d\vect{z}, \\
  \text{conditional:} \quad & p(\vect{z} \mid \x) = \frac{p(\x, \vect{z})}{p(\x)} \qquad (p(\x) > 0), \\
  \text{Bayes' rule:} \quad & p(\vect{z} \mid \x) = \frac{p(\x \mid \vect{z})\, p(\vect{z})}{\int p(\x \mid \vect{z}')\, p(\vect{z}')\, d\vect{z}'} .
\end{align*}
Independence means $p(\x, \vect{z}) = p(\x)\,p(\vect{z})$, equivalently $p(\vect{z} \mid \x) = p(\vect{z})$.
Expectations are integrals against the density, $\E[g(\X)] = \int g(\x)\,p(\x)\,d\x$; the mean is $\vmu = \E[\X]$
and the covariance matrix is $\Sigma = \E[(\X - \vmu)(\X - \vmu)\T]$, with variances on its diagonal.

\section{Four things we do with a distribution}\label{sec:operations}

Whether the distribution is a probability table, a Gaussian or a neural network with billions of parameters, the
same four operations recur.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sample}: generate new data $\x \sim p$. This is what a generative model is for.
\item \textbf{Evaluate}: compute $p(\x)$ (the \emph{likelihood} of a configuration), possibly after marginalising
over unobserved variables. Low likelihood flags unusual data.
\item \textbf{Infer}: given some variables, predict others --- the most probable value or the conditional mean
$\E[\vect{Z} \mid \X = \x]$. Supervised learning is inference of $Y$ from $\X$.
\item \textbf{Learn}: choose the parameters $\theta$ of a family $p_\theta$ from data. The standard principle is
\emph{maximum likelihood},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mle}
  \hat\theta = \argmax_\theta \prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(\x_i) = \argmin_\theta \; -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(\x_i),
\end{equation}
the product being justified by the i.i.d.\ assumption. The right-hand side is the average negative log-likelihood,
the density-estimation loss of Note~1, Table~2.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[a categorical variable]
The number $X \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ of reconstructed jets in a simplified event selection is described by three numbers
$\pi_0, \pi_1, \pi_2$ with $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$. \emph{Sampling} draws $u$ uniform on $[0,1)$ and returns the first
$k$ with $\pi_0 + \dots + \pi_k > u$. \emph{Learning} by maximum likelihood from $n$ events with counts
$n_0, n_1, n_2$ gives $\hat\pi_k = n_k / n$, the observed frequencies (Exercise~E1).
\end{example}

\begin{modern}{a language model is a categorical distribution}
A large language model defines, for every prefix of text, a categorical distribution over a vocabulary of
$V \approx 10^5$ tokens. The network outputs scores (``logits'') $z_1, \dots, z_V$ and the probabilities are
$\pi_k = e^{z_k/T} / \sum_j e^{z_j/T}$, where the \emph{temperature} $T = 1$ during training. All four operations
appear: training is maximum likelihood \eqref{eq:mle} on the next token, i.e.\ minimising cross-entropy; generating
text is repeated sampling, with $T < 1$ concentrating mass on likely tokens and $T \to 0$ giving the single most
probable token; evaluating the likelihood of a text gives its \emph{perplexity}, $\exp$ of the average negative
log-likelihood per token; and answering a question is inference, conditioning on the prompt. The example above
--- learned frequencies from counts --- is exactly what such a model would do if it had no parameters to share
between contexts.
\end{modern}

\section{The univariate Gaussian}\label{sec:gauss1d}

The Gaussian (normal) density with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gauss1d}
  \Normal(x; \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).
\end{equation}
It is a symmetric bump centred at $\mu$ with width set by the standard deviation $\sigma$
(Figure~\ref{fig:gauss1d}). About $68.3\%$, $95.4\%$ and $99.7\%$ of the probability lies within one, two and three
standard deviations of the mean; physicists' ``$5\sigma$'' discovery threshold corresponds to a one-sided tail
probability of $2.9 \times 10^{-7}$. Any Gaussian is a shifted and scaled standard one:
$X = \mu + \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)$.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.52\linewidth]{figures/gaussian_1d.pdf}
\caption{Gaussian densities. Doubling $\sigma$ halves the peak height, since the area stays one.}
\label{fig:gauss1d}
\end{figure}

Why this particular shape? The central limit theorem says that a sum of many independent contributions with finite
variance is approximately Gaussian, whatever the individual distributions. Measurement noise built from many small
effects is therefore often close to Gaussian. A second reason is that, among all densities with a given mean and
variance, the Gaussian has maximal entropy: it assumes nothing beyond those two numbers.

\paragraph{Gaussian noise and squared error} Suppose targets are generated as $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2)$. Then $-\log p(y \mid x) = \frac{(y - f(x))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac12
\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$, and maximising the likelihood of a regression dataset over $f$ is the same as minimising the sum
of squared errors. The squared loss of Note~1, Table~2, is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise; heavier-tailed
noise calls for other losses (Exercise~E3).

\begin{physics}{$\chi^2$ fits are Gaussian likelihoods}
Fitting a model $f_\theta$ to measurements $y_i \pm \sigma_i$ by minimising $\chi^2 = \sum_i (y_i -
f_\theta(x_i))^2 / \sigma_i^2$ is maximum likelihood with independent Gaussian errors of known widths. The familiar
rule ``$\Delta\chi^2 = 1$ gives the $1\sigma$ interval'' is the statement that $-2\log$ likelihood is quadratic
near its minimum~\cite{cowan1998}. When errors are correlated, the sum becomes the quadratic form of the next
section, with the covariance matrix of the measurements.
\end{physics}

\section{The multivariate Gaussian}\label{sec:gaussnd}

For $\x \in \R^d$, the Gaussian with mean $\vmu \in \R^d$ and symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix
$\Sigma \in \R^{d \times d}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussnd}
  \Normal(\x; \vmu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \lvert\Sigma\rvert^{1/2}}
  \exp\!\left( -\tfrac12 (\x - \vmu)\T \Sigma^{-1} (\x - \vmu) \right),
\end{equation}
where $\lvert\Sigma\rvert$ is the determinant. The prefactor only normalises the density; all the shape is in the
exponent. The quantity
\[
  \Delta^2(\x) = (\x - \vmu)\T \Sigma^{-1} (\x - \vmu)
\]
is the squared \emph{Mahalanobis distance} of $\x$ from the mean: Euclidean distance after the data have been
rescaled so that every direction has unit variance. Surfaces of constant density are the ellipsoids
$\Delta^2 = \text{const}$.

\paragraph{Geometry through the eigendecomposition} Write $\Sigma = \sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i \vect{v}_i \vect{v}_i\T$
with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vect{v}_i$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$. In the rotated coordinates
$u_i = \vect{v}_i\T(\x - \vmu)$ the exponent becomes $\sum_i u_i^2 / \lambda_i$, so the density factorises into $d$
independent one-dimensional Gaussians with variances $\lambda_i$. The ellipsoid axes point along the eigenvectors
with half-lengths proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, and $\lvert\Sigma\rvert = \prod_i \lambda_i$ measures the
squared volume of the ellipsoid. Three families are common (Figure~\ref{fig:gauss2d}):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.18\linewidth}L{0.24\linewidth}L{0.22\linewidth}L{0.26\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Family & Covariance & Free parameters in $\Sigma$ & Shape \\
\midrule
Isotropic & $\sigma^2 I$ & $1$ & spheres \\
Diagonal & $\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_d^2)$ & $d$ & axis-aligned ellipsoids \\
Full & any positive-definite $\Sigma$ & $d(d+1)/2$ & arbitrary ellipsoids \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/gaussian_2d.pdf}
\caption{Samples (grey) and the one- and two-$\sigma$ contour ellipses of three Gaussians in $d = 2$. Arrows are the
eigenvectors scaled by $2\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The isotropic case has no preferred direction; the full covariance

\label{fig:gauss2d}
\end{figure}

The choice is a choice of model size. For $d = 1000$ a full covariance has about half a million parameters, which
$n = 500$ examples cannot determine; Note~4 calls this a question of \emph{capacity}.

\paragraph{Closed under marginalising and conditioning} Split $\x = (\x_a, \x_b)$ and partition
$\vmu$ and $\Sigma$ accordingly. Every marginal of a Gaussian is Gaussian --- just keep the corresponding entries,
$\x_a \sim \Normal(\vmu_a, \Sigma_{aa})$ --- and every conditional is Gaussian,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conditional}
  \x_a \mid \x_b \sim \Normal\!\big( \vmu_a + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\x_b - \vmu_b),\;
  \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba} \big).
\end{equation}
The conditional mean is linear in the observed part and the conditional covariance does not depend on it. This is
inference in closed form: if two detector channels are correlated, reading one sharpens the prediction for the
other by an amount set by the correlation. Formula \eqref{eq:conditional} underlies the Kalman filter used for
particle-track reconstruction and spacecraft navigation, and Gaussian-process regression.

\section{Sampling: from white noise to anything Gaussian}\label{sec:sampling}

To sample $\Normal(\vmu, \Sigma)$, factor $\Sigma = LL\T$ (Cholesky decomposition, $L$ lower triangular), draw
$\vect{z} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, I)$ --- $d$ independent standard normals --- and return
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reparam}
  \x = \vmu + L \vect{z}.
\end{equation}
Indeed $\E[\x] = \vmu$ and $\Cov(\x) = L\,\E[\vect{z}\vect{z}\T]\,L\T = LL\T = \Sigma$, and a linear map of a
Gaussian is Gaussian.

\begin{modern}{Gaussian noise drives generative models}
Equation \eqref{eq:reparam} separates the randomness ($\vect{z}$) from the parameters ($\vmu, L$). Variational
autoencoders exploit this ``reparameterisation trick'' to differentiate through sampling~\cite{kingma2014}.
Diffusion models, behind many of today's image generators, go further. Their \emph{forward process}
gradually turns data into noise: with $\bar\alpha_t$ decreasing from $1$ to $0$,
\[
  \x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, \x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \vect{z}, \qquad \vect{z} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, I),
\]
so $\x_t \mid \x_0 \sim \Normal(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\x_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I)$, an isotropic Gaussian
(Figure~\ref{fig:diffusion}). A neural network is trained to predict $\vect{z}$ from $\x_t$; running the process
backwards from pure noise then generates new samples~\cite{ho2020,song2021}. Everything in this section --- isotropic
covariances, scaling and shifting, sampling by \eqref{eq:reparam} --- is used literally.
\end{modern}

\section{Learning the parameters}\label{sec:learn}

Given $\x_1, \dots, \x_n \in \R^d$, the maximum-likelihood estimates of a Gaussian are the empirical mean and
covariance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mlegauss}
  \hat{\vmu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \x_i, \qquad
  \hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\x_i - \hat{\vmu})(\x_i - \hat{\vmu})\T = \frac{1}{n} X_c\T X_c ,
\end{equation}
where $X_c$ is the design matrix of Note~1 with $\hat{\vmu}$ subtracted from every row. In one dimension the derivation
takes two lines: setting the derivatives of $\sum_i \big[(x_i - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2) + \frac12 \log \sigma^2\big]$ with
respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ to zero gives $\hat\mu = \bar x$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$.
The variance estimate is slightly biased, $\E[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, which is why statistics
software often divides by $n - 1$; for the sample sizes of machine learning the difference is negligible.

\paragraph{When $n$ is not much larger than $d$} The matrix $\hat\Sigma = X_c\T X_c / n$ has rank at most $n - 1$, so for
$n \le d$ it is singular: the fitted Gaussian is flat in some directions and \eqref{eq:gaussnd} cannot be evaluated.
Even for $n$ somewhat larger than $d$ its small eigenvalues are badly underestimated. The usual remedies are to
restrict the family (diagonal or isotropic) or to \emph{shrink}, $\hat\Sigma + \epsilon I$ --- the simplest
instance of the regularisation discussed in Note~4.

\paragraph{Computing log-densities} In practice one evaluates $\log \Normal(\x; \vmu, \Sigma)$, never the density
itself, which underflows in high dimension. With $\Sigma = LL\T$, solve the triangular system $L\vect{u} = \x -
\vmu$; then $\Delta^2 = \norm{\vect{u}}^2$ and $\log\lvert\Sigma\rvert = 2\sum_i \log L_{ii}$. No matrix is ever
inverted.

\begin{verbatim}
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular
def gaussian_logpdf(X, mu, Sigma):            # X: (n, d)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
    U = solve_triangular(L, (X - mu).T, lower=True)     # (d, n)
    d = X.shape[1]
    log_det_half = np.log(np.diag(L)).sum()             # = 0.5 log|Sigma|
    return -0.5 * (U**2).sum(0) - log_det_half - 0.5 * d * np.log(2 * np.pi)
\end{verbatim}

\begin{modern}{Mahalanobis distance as an anomaly detector}
A trained classifier can be fooled by inputs unlike anything it has seen. A simple and strong defence fits a
Gaussian to the network's internal feature vectors for each class (with a shared covariance) and flags a new input
whose features have a large Mahalanobis distance to every class mean~\cite{lee2018}. The same recipe --- fit a
Gaussian to normal operation, alarm on large $\Delta^2$ --- is used to monitor detectors and industrial processes;
under the model, $\Delta^2$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $d$ degrees of freedom, which sets the alarm threshold.
\end{modern}

\begin{keyidea}[Summary]
A distribution is given by a c.d.f., a probability table or a density; marginals, conditionals and Bayes' rule
relate its parts. We sample from, evaluate, infer with and learn distributions, and maximum likelihood is the
learning principle --- for language models as for $\chi^2$ fits. The multivariate Gaussian is described by a mean
and a covariance whose eigenvectors and eigenvalues give the axes and widths of its elliptical contours. It is
closed under marginalising and conditioning, sampled as $\vmu + L\vect{z}$, and fitted by the empirical mean and
covariance, which require care when $d$ is comparable to $n$.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{exercises}
\item Show that the maximum-likelihood estimate of a categorical distribution from counts $n_1, \dots, n_m$ is
$\hat\pi_k = n_k / n$. (Maximise $\sum_k n_k \log \pi_k$ subject to $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier.)
What does it predict for a category never observed, and why is this a problem for a language model?
\item Verify that \eqref{eq:gauss1d} integrates to one and has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.
\item If the noise has the Laplace density $p(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{2b} e^{-|\varepsilon|/b}$, which loss does
maximum likelihood minimise? Which estimate of a constant $\theta$ minimises $\sum_i |y_i - \theta|$, and why is it
less sensitive to outliers than the mean?
\item For $d = 2$, $\vmu = 0$ and $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ with $|\rho| < 1$,
find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and use \eqref{eq:conditional} to compute $p(x_1 \mid x_2)$. How
much does observing $x_2$ reduce the variance of $x_1$ when $\rho = 0.9$?
\item Let $\vect{z} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, I_d)$. Show that $\E\norm{\vect{z}}^2 = d$ and $\Var\norm{\vect{z}}^2 = 2d$.
Conclude that for large $d$ almost all samples lie near a sphere of radius $\sqrt d$, far from the mode at the origin.
(Note~3 returns to this.)
\item Implement sampling by \eqref{eq:reparam} and \texttt{gaussian\_logpdf}. Draw $n$ samples from a Gaussian in $d = 50$
with a random full covariance, refit by \eqref{eq:mlegauss}, and plot the average test log-likelihood against $n$ for
the full, diagonal and shrunk ($\hat\Sigma + \epsilon I$) estimates. Which wins for small $n$?
\end{exercises}
