\NoteTitle{4}{2}{Risk, generalisation\\ and model selection}{Risk and model selection}{%
This note turns the informal goal ``predict well on new data'' into mathematics and into a working procedure. It
defines expected and empirical risk, proves that a test-set estimate is unbiased and computes its standard error,
explains why training error is optimistic, and shows how to estimate generalisation honestly with hold-out data,
cross-validation, leave-one-out, sequential and nested validation. Capacity, under- and overfitting, the
approximation--estimation and bias--variance decompositions and regularisation explain how to choose a model; grid and
random search organise the choice. Every procedure comes with pseudocode and its cost, and every idea with a small

\section{A learning algorithm has three ingredients}\label{sec:ingredients}

Almost every learning algorithm combines, explicitly or implicitly,
\begin{enumerate}
\item a \textbf{family of functions} $\F = \{f_\theta\}$ indexed by parameters $\theta$;
\item a \textbf{loss} $L(f(\x), y)$ that measures the cost of predicting $f(\x)$ when the truth is $y$
(Note~1, Table~2);
\item an \textbf{optimisation procedure} that searches $\F$ for a function with small average loss on the training
data.
\end{enumerate}
The families met in this course grow from the very small to the very large. A histogram classifier (Note~3)
predicts one value per cell, a staircase function. A $k$-nearest-neighbour ($k$-NN) predictor answers a query $\x$
with the average target (regression) or the majority label (classification) of the $k$ training points nearest to
$\x$; $1$-NN is the case $k = 1$. It and the Parzen predictor (Note~5) are weighted sums of kernels centred on the
training points, and so are the kernel machines of Note~10. A fit of a calibration curve uses polynomials, and a linear regression or
classifier (Notes~8 and~9) uses affine functions $f(\x) = \vect{w}\T\x + b$ --- with the constant $f(\x) = b$ as the
degenerate case; a decision tree (Note~3) is a histogram whose cells adapt to the data. A neural network is the extreme: its architecture fixes the family, its weights pick a member.
Choosing the family is the most consequential decision in a project, and it is the subject of this note.

The three ingredients are independent choices. The same family can be trained with different losses, and the same
loss can be minimised by different optimisers --- in closed form, by enumeration or by gradient descent (Note~7).
The smallest possible example already shows how much the loss matters.

\begin{worked}{the best constant under three losses}
Take the family of constants, $f(\x) = b$, and the training targets $y = 1, 2, 2, 3, 7$ ($n = 5$). The training
objective is $J(b) = \frac1n \sum_i L(b, y_i)$.

\emph{Squared loss.} $J(b) = \frac15 \sum_i (b - y_i)^2$ and
\[
  J'(b) = \frac{2}{n} \sum_i (b - y_i) = 2\,(b - \bar y) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat b = \bar y = 3 .
\]
$J''(b) = 2 > 0$, so this is a minimum. The training risk is
$\frac15(4 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 16) = 4.4$.

\emph{Absolute loss.} $J(b) = \frac15 \sum_i |b - y_i|$. Away from the data points the derivative of $|b - y_i|$ is
$+1$ if $b > y_i$ and $-1$ if $b < y_i$, so
$J'(b) = \frac1n\big[\#\{y_i < b\} - \#\{y_i > b\}\big]$. It changes sign where as many targets lie below $b$ as
above: $\hat b$ is the \emph{median}, $2$, with training risk $\frac15(1 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 5) = 1.4$. The mean, $b = 3$,
would give $1.6$; the median would give squared risk $5.4$ instead of $4.4$.

\emph{0--1 loss} (for class labels). $J(b)$ is the fraction of labels different from $b$, minimised by the most
frequent label --- the \emph{majority class}.

Each loss picks a different ``centre'' of the data: the mean is pulled by the outlier $7$, the median is not.
Which is right depends on what the prediction is for, not on the data.
\end{worked}

\section{Measuring quality}\label{sec:quality}

What we ultimately care about is rarely the loss the algorithm minimises. Take a jet tagger in a collider analysis.
What matters in the end is the precision of the measurement it serves --- the uncertainty on a cross-section, or the
expected significance of a search. One level down is a metric specific to the field, such as the background rejection
at a fixed signal efficiency. Below that sit generic metrics (error rate, mean squared error), and at the bottom the
loss the training actually minimises, usually a cross-entropy. Each level is a proxy for the one above it, and the
gaps between them deserve attention.

\paragraph{Surrogate losses} The classification error $\ind{f(\x) \ne y}$ is piecewise constant in the parameters,
so its gradient is zero almost everywhere; and when the classes are not linearly separable, no efficient algorithm is
known for finding the linear classifier with the fewest training errors (the problem is NP-hard). One therefore trains
a real-valued score $g$ and minimises a continuous, convex \emph{surrogate} with two properties: it upper-bounds (or
closely tracks) the 0--1 error, and its slope is non-zero wherever a decision is wrong or only barely right --- exactly
where the 0--1 loss is flat but the decision should change --- so that a (sub)gradient points towards fewer errors.
(The hinge $\max(0, 1 - t\,g)$ below has a kink at $t\,g = 1$ and is flat beyond it, where decisions are already confidently right.) With $t = 2y - 1 \in \{\pm 1\}$ for binary
labels, the four common choices are:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.16\linewidth}L{0.30\linewidth}L{0.27\linewidth}L{0.18\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Surrogate & Loss on one example & Output $\to$ decision & Used by \\
\midrule
hinge & $\max(0,\, 1 - t\,g(\x))$ & score $g$; \ $\ind{g > 0}$ & SVM (Note~9) \\
binary cross-entropy & $-y\log g - (1-y)\log(1-g)$ & $g \approx P(Y = 1 \mid \x)$; \ $\ind{g > \tfrac12}$ & logistic regression (Note~9) \\
multiclass margin & $\max\big(0,\, 1 + \max_{k \ne y} g_k - g_y\big)$ & scores $g_1, \dots, g_m$; \ $\argmax_k g_k$ & multiclass SVM \\
cross-entropy & $-\log g_y(\x)$ & $g_k \approx P(Y = k \mid \x)$; \ $\argmax_k g_k$ & softmax classifiers, networks \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The cross-entropies are negative log-likelihoods of a categorical model (Note~2, Section~2). Probabilistic outputs
have an extra benefit: the threshold can be moved after training to trade one kind of error for another.

\paragraph{Losses as functions of the margin} For a binary score write $z = t\,g(\x)$, the \emph{margin}: $z > 0$
means a correct decision, $z < 0$ a wrong one, and $|z|$ the confidence. Figure~\ref{fig:surrogates} plots the losses
against $z$. If the score is a log-odds, $g = \log\frac{P(Y=1\mid\x)}{P(Y=0\mid\x)}$, then
$P(Y = 1 \mid \x) = 1/(1 + e^{-g})$ and $P(Y = 0 \mid \x) = 1 - P(Y = 1 \mid \x) = 1/(1 + e^{g})$. For $y = 1$
($t = +1$) the cross-entropy is $-\log P(Y = 1 \mid \x) = \log(1 + e^{-g})$; for $y = 0$ ($t = -1$) it is
$-\log P(Y = 0 \mid \x) = \log(1 + e^{g})$. Both cases are $\log(1 + e^{-t g}) = \log(1 + e^{-z})$. Measured in bits (logarithm to base~2) it equals $1$ at $z = 0$ and decreases, so it is
\emph{at least} $1$ whenever the decision is wrong; the hinge $1 - z$ is also at least $1$ there. Both surrogates are
therefore upper bounds on the 0--1 loss, and driving them down drives the error down. Unlike the 0--1 loss they keep
penalising correct but unconfident decisions ($0 < z < 1$), which is what gives them useful gradients. The squared loss
$(1 - z)^2$ is an upper bound too, but it also penalises decisions that are ``too right'' ($z > 1$), a weakness
discussed in Note~9, Section~2. Since $t^2 = 1$, $(t - g)^2 = t^2(1 - t g)^2 = (1 - z)^2$: this is the squared loss on
the \emph{score}, not the squared loss on a probability (the Brier score) used in the worked example below.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.46\linewidth]{figures/n4_surrogates.pdf}
\caption{Binary classification losses as functions of the margin $z = t\,g(\x)$. Hinge, cross-entropy in bits and
squared loss all lie above the 0--1 loss for $z \le 0$.}

\end{figure}

\begin{worked}{one prediction table, three losses}
A classifier outputs $g_i \approx P(Y = 1 \mid \x_i)$ for five validation examples. The decision is
$\ind{g_i > \frac12}$; the squared loss is applied to the probability, $(g_i - y_i)^2$ (this is called the
\emph{Brier score}); the cross-entropy uses the natural logarithm.

\begin{tabular}{@{}cccccc@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
1 & 0.9 & 1 & 0 & $0.1^2 = 0.01$ & $-\ln 0.9 = 0.105$ \\
2 & 0.6 & 1 & 0 & $0.4^2 = 0.16$ & $-\ln 0.6 = 0.511$ \\
3 & 0.3 & 0 & 0 & $0.3^2 = 0.09$ & $-\ln 0.7 = 0.357$ \\
4 & 0.2 & 1 & 1 & $0.8^2 = 0.64$ & $-\ln 0.2 = 1.609$ \\

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The three numbers measure different things. The error rate counts mistakes only. The cross-entropy also charges
example~2, which is correct but hesitant, and it charges the confident mistake of example~4 ($1.609$) more than the
milder mistake of example~5 ($1.204$); a completely undecided prediction, $g = 0.5$, would have cost
$-\ln 0.5 = 0.693$ whatever the label. In bits the cross-entropies are
$0.152, 0.737, 0.515, 2.322, 1.737$: the two wrong examples cost more than one bit each, as the margin argument above
requires. (In margin form the score is the log-odds $s_i = \ln\frac{g_i}{1 - g_i}$ and $z_i = t_i s_i$; example~4 has
$s = \ln 0.25 = -1.39$ and $t = +1$, so $z = -1.39 < 0$, and example~5 has $z = -\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} = -0.85$.) A model with the same 0--1 error but probabilities closer to the true labels (more confident when right,
less confident when wrong) would have a lower cross-entropy, which matters whenever the threshold will be moved later.
\end{worked}

\paragraph{Precision, recall and the F-score} When one class is rare --- a signal among much background --- the error
rate hides what matters. Count the four outcomes of a binary decision on a test set (``positive'' = class~$1$):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & predicted $1$ & predicted $0$ \\
\midrule
true $1$ & TP (true positives) & FN (false negatives) \\
true $0$ & FP (false positives) & TN (true negatives) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This table is the \emph{confusion matrix}. \emph{Precision} $= \mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})$ is the
fraction of predicted positives that are real; \emph{recall} $= \mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})$ is the
fraction of real positives that are found. The \emph{F-score} $F_1$ is their harmonic mean,
$F_1 = 2\,\mathrm{prec}\cdot\mathrm{rec}/(\mathrm{prec} + \mathrm{rec}) = 2\,\mathrm{TP}/(2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}
+ \mathrm{FN})$, which is small as soon as either of the two is small. Moving the decision threshold trades one for the
other.

\begin{worked}{high accuracy, mediocre recall}
On $1000$ test events with $60$ true positives a classifier gives $\mathrm{TP} = 40$, $\mathrm{FN} = 20$,
$\mathrm{FP} = 10$, $\mathrm{TN} = 930$. Accuracy $= (40 + 930)/1000 = 0.97$. Precision $= 40/50 = 0.80$, recall
$= 40/60 = 0.667$ and $F_1 = 2 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 0.667/(0.8 + 0.667) = 80/110 = 0.727$. The accuracy looks excellent,
but a third of the positives are missed --- and the trivial rule ``always predict $0$'' already reaches accuracy
$940/1000 = 0.94$ with recall $0$ and an undefined precision.
\end{worked}

\begin{physics}{efficiency, rejection and the ROC curve}
A trigger or tagger outputs a score $g(\x)$, and the analysis keeps events with $g(\x) > \tau$. The \emph{signal
efficiency} is the fraction of true signal events kept --- the recall --- and the \emph{background rejection} the
fraction of background events removed; the \emph{purity} of the selected sample is the precision. Lowering $\tau$
raises the efficiency and lowers the rejection; the curve traced by all thresholds is the ROC (receiver operating
characteristic) curve. The
best threshold is fixed by the physics goal --- for instance, the expected discovery significance --- not by the
training loss. Classifiers in physics are therefore typically trained with cross-entropy and then judged at the working
point, or over the part of the ROC curve, that the analysis needs.
\end{physics}

\begin{modern}{training loss versus what we want}
Language models are pre-trained to minimise next-token cross-entropy, yet they are judged on answering questions,
writing code and following instructions. Besides supervised instruction tuning, one of the main tools for closing that
gap is fine-tuning on human preferences: a second model learns which of two answers people prefer, and the language
model is optimised for that learned reward~\cite{ouyang2022}. It is the hierarchy above, climbed one level at a time.
\end{modern}

\section{Expected risk and empirical risk}\label{sec:risk}

Assume, as in Note~1, that examples are i.i.d.\ draws from an unknown $P(\X, Y)$. The \emph{expected risk}
(generalisation error) of a fixed predictor $f$ is its average loss on future data,
\[
  \Risk(f) = \E_{(\X, Y) \sim P}\big[L(f(\X), Y)\big],
\]
and its \emph{empirical risk} on a finite set $D$ is
\[
  \ERisk(f, D) = \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{(\x, y) \in D} L(f(\x), y).
\]
We cannot compute $\Risk(f)$, because $P$ is unknown, but if $f$ was chosen \emph{without using} $D$, then
$\ERisk(f, D)$ is an honest estimate of it. The next subsection makes ``honest'' precise.

\subsection{The test-set estimate: unbiased, with a computable error bar}\label{sec:unbiased}

Let $\Dtest = \{(\x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^{n'}$ be drawn i.i.d.\ from $P$, independently of everything used to build $f$.
(Throughout this note $n'$ is the number of test or held-out examples, also written $n_{\mathrm{test}}$ or
$n_{\mathrm{valid}}$ when the role of the set matters; $m$ keeps its course-wide meaning, the number
of classes.)
Write $L_j = L(f(\x_j), y_j)$. Because $f$ is fixed with respect to $\Dtest$, the $L_j$ are i.i.d.\ copies of the
random variable $L(f(\X), Y)$, with mean $\Risk(f)$ and some variance $\sigma_L^2$.

\emph{Mean.} By linearity of expectation,
\[
  \E\big[\ERisk(f, \Dtest)\big] = \E\Big[\frac{1}{n'} \sum_{j=1}^{n'} L_j\Big] = \frac{1}{n'} \sum_{j=1}^{n'} \E[L_j]
  = \frac{1}{n'} \cdot n'\,\Risk(f) = \Risk(f) .
\]
The estimate is \emph{unbiased}: on average over test sets it neither over- nor underestimates.

\emph{Variance.} Independent terms have zero covariance, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:
\[
  \Var\big[\ERisk(f, \Dtest)\big] = \frac{1}{(n')^2} \Var\Big[\sum_{j} L_j\Big] = \frac{1}{(n')^2} \sum_{j} \Var[L_j]
  = \frac{\sigma_L^2}{n'} .
\]
The \emph{standard error} is $\sigma_L / \sqrt{n'}$: to halve it, quadruple the test set. As $n' \to \infty$ the estimate
converges to $\Risk(f)$ (law of large numbers), and for moderate $n'$ it is approximately Gaussian (central limit
theorem), so $\ERisk \pm 2\,\sigma_L/\sqrt{n'}$ is roughly a 95\% interval.

\emph{The 0--1 loss.} If $f$ errs with probability $p = \Risk(f)$, then $L_j \in \{0, 1\}$ is a Bernoulli variable.
Since $L_j^2 = L_j$, $\sigma_L^2 = \E[L_j^2] - (\E L_j)^2 = p - p^2 = p(1 - p)$, and the standard error of a test
error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:se}
  \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n'}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n'}} .
\end{equation}
With $p = 10\%$ and $n' = 1000$ test examples that is about $\pm 0.95$ percentage points: a difference of this size
between two models can be produced by test-set noise alone. Requiring $2\,\mathrm{SE} \le \delta$ gives the test size
needed to measure \emph{one} error rate to within $\pm\delta$: $n' \ge 4p(1-p)/\delta^2$, which for $p = 0.1$ and
$\delta = 1$ point is $3600$. To resolve a \emph{difference} $\delta$ between two models evaluated on independent test
sets the variance doubles, so $n' \ge 8p(1-p)/\delta^2 = 7200$; evaluating both on the same examples (next example)
usually needs far fewer.

\begin{worked}{is model B really better than model A}
Two classifiers are evaluated on the \emph{same} $n' = 1000$ test examples. A makes $80$ errors ($\hat p_A = 8.0\%$),
B makes $65$ ($\hat p_B = 6.5\%$). From \eqref{eq:se}, $\mathrm{SE}_A = \sqrt{0.08 \cdot 0.92/1000} = 0.86\%$ and
$\mathrm{SE}_B = 0.78\%$.

\emph{Naive comparison.} Treating the two estimates as independent, the difference $1.5$ points has standard error
$\sqrt{0.86^2 + 0.78^2} = 1.16$ points: only $1.3$ standard errors, not convincing.

\emph{Paired comparison.} Both models saw the same examples, so compare them example by example. Suppose $45$ examples
are missed by both, $35$ only by A and $20$ only by B. Let $\Delta_j = L_{A,j} - L_{B,j} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ be the
difference on example~$j$; then $\hat p_A - \hat p_B = \bar\Delta = \frac{1}{n'}\sum_j \Delta_j = (35 - 20)/1000 =
0.015$. The $\Delta_j$ are i.i.d., so by the same argument as for $\ERisk$ above, $\Var(\bar\Delta) =
\Var(\Delta)/n'$. To estimate $\Var(\Delta) = \E[\Delta^2] - (\E\Delta)^2$, use the sample mean of the squares,
$\overline{\Delta^2} = \frac{1}{n'}\sum_j \Delta_j^2$. Since $\Delta_j^2 = 1$ exactly when one model is wrong and the
other right,
\[
  \widehat{\Var}(\Delta) = \overline{\Delta^2} - \bar\Delta^2 = \frac{35 + 20}{1000} - 0.015^2 = 0.0548,
  \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar\Delta) = \sqrt{0.0548/1000} = 0.74 \text{ points}.
\]
(Dividing by $n' - 1$ instead of $n'$, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:holdout}, makes no visible difference at $n' = 1000$.)
The difference is now $2.0$ standard errors --- right at the rough 95\% threshold above: borderline but real evidence,
where the naive comparison ($1.3$ standard errors) showed nothing. The examples both models get wrong (or right)
have $\Delta_j = 0$ and carry no information about the difference; pairing removes their contribution to the noise. Always compare models on the same test set, and
compare them pairwise.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Empirical risk minimisation}

Since the expected risk is out of reach, learning minimises its estimate on the training set,
\[
  \hat f = \argmin_{f \in \F} \ERisk(f, \Dtrain).
\]
This principle is \emph{empirical risk minimisation} (ERM), and it is the training phase of almost every method in
the course. Algorithm~\ref{alg:erm} writes it generically; the three branches correspond to the three ways the
optimisation ingredient of Section~\ref{sec:ingredients} is realised in practice.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Empirical risk minimisation (generic)}
\label{alg:erm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require family $\{f_\theta\}$, loss $L$, training set $\Dtrain = \{(\x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, optional penalty $\lambda\,\Omega(\theta)$
\Statex \Comment{the penalty is explained in Section~\ref{sec:regularisation}; $\lambda = 0$ means no penalty}
\Ensure fitted parameters $\hat\theta$
\Function{ERM}{$\Dtrain$}
  \State $J(\theta) \gets \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n L(f_\theta(\x_i), y_i) + \lambda\,\Omega(\theta)$ \Comment{training objective}
  \If{$\nabla J(\theta) = 0$ can be solved in closed form}
    \State $\hat\theta \gets$ its solution \Comment{mean, least squares (Note~8)}
  \ElsIf{the family is finite, $\{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M\}$}
    \State $\hat\theta \gets \argmin_{1 \le j \le M} J(\theta_j)$ \Comment{enumerate every candidate}
  \Else
    \State $\hat\theta \gets$ an iterative minimiser of $J$, e.g.\ gradient descent \Comment{Note~7}
  \EndIf
  \State \Return $\hat\theta$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{empirical risk minimisation}
Let $c$ be the cost of one prediction $f_\theta(\x)$ ($c = O(d)$ for a linear model in $d$ features) and $q$ the
number of parameters. \emph{One evaluation of $J$}: $O(nc)$, one prediction and one loss per example. \emph{Finite
family}: $O(Mnc)$, every candidate on every example. \emph{Iterative}: $O(N_{\mathrm{ep}}\,nc)$ for $N_{\mathrm{ep}}$ full passes (epochs), since a gradient
costs about as much as the objective itself (Note~7); stochastic gradients replace $n$ by a mini-batch size.
\emph{Closed form}: problem-specific, e.g.\ $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ for least squares, the $d^3$ coming from solving a
$d \times d$ linear system (Note~8). \emph{Prediction}: $c$ per query. \emph{Memory}: $O(q)$ for the model plus the
$O(nd)$ training data during training.
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Why training error is optimistic}\label{sec:optimism}

Now $\hat f$ \emph{was} chosen using $\Dtrain$, specifically to make $\ERisk(\hat f, \Dtrain)$ small, so the
argument of Section~\ref{sec:unbiased} no longer applies: the terms $L(\hat f(\x_i), y_i)$ are not independent of
$\hat f$. The bias always goes the same way. Let $f_{\F} = \argmin_{f \in \F} \Risk(f)$ be the best member of the
family; it is a fixed function, so its training error is unbiased. Then, for every training set,
\[
  \ERisk(\hat f, \Dtrain) \le \ERisk(f_{\F}, \Dtrain) \quad (\hat f \text{ minimises } \ERisk)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \Risk(f_{\F}) \le \Risk(\hat f) \quad (f_{\F} \text{ minimises } \Risk).
\]
Taking expectations over $\Dtrain$ and using $\E[\ERisk(f_{\F}, \Dtrain)] = \Risk(f_{\F})$,
\[
  \E\big[\ERisk(\hat f, \Dtrain)\big] \;\le\; \Risk(f_{\F}) \;\le\; \E\big[\Risk(\hat f)\big] .
\]
Here $\Risk(\hat f)$ is itself a random number, because $\hat f$ depends on the random training set; the outer
expectation averages over training sets. On average the training error of the trained model is below its true risk --- often drastically, as the histogram
classifier of Note~3 showed (5.2\% training error, 34.6\% test error).

\begin{worked}{the optimism of the sample mean}
Fit the constant predictor $\hat b = \bar y$ to $n$ i.i.d.\ targets with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, with
squared loss. Using $y_i - \bar y = (y_i - \mu) - (\bar y - \mu)$ and $\sum_i (y_i - \mu) = n(\bar y - \mu)$,
\[
  \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 = \sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 - 2(\bar y - \mu)\, n(\bar y - \mu) + n(\bar y - \mu)^2
  = \sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar y - \mu)^2 .
\]
With $\E[(y_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\E[(\bar y - \mu)^2] = \Var(\bar y) = \sigma^2/n$, the expected sum is
$\E[\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2] = n\sigma^2 - n\cdot\sigma^2/n = n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2$, so
\[
  \E[\text{training error}] = \frac1n\big(n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2\big) = \Big(1 - \frac1n\Big)\sigma^2 .
\]
For a new target $Y$, independent of the training set, the cross term vanishes and
\[
  \E\big[(Y - \bar y)^2\big] = \E\big[(Y - \mu)^2\big] + \E\big[(\bar y - \mu)^2\big] = \Big(1 + \frac1n\Big)\sigma^2 .
\]
With $n = 5$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$: training error $0.8$ on average, true risk $1.2$ (a simulation over $200\,000$
training sets gives $0.798$ and $1.199$). The gap $2\sigma^2/n$ is the price of one fitted parameter. For least
squares with $q$ fitted coefficients, with the error measured on new targets drawn at the same inputs
$\x_1, \dots, \x_n$, the same calculation gives a gap of
$2q\sigma^2/n$~\cite{hastie2009}: optimism grows with capacity and shrinks with data.
\end{worked}

\begin{keyidea}[Never evaluate on data used for choosing]
$\ERisk(f, D)$ is an honest estimate of $\Risk(f)$ only if $D$ played no part in choosing $f$ --- not in fitting the
parameters, not in choosing hyperparameters, not in pre-processing, not in deciding which model to report.
\end{keyidea}

Learning therefore has two phases. In the \emph{training phase} ERM fits $\hat f$; memorising $\Dtrain$ is not the
goal. In the \emph{test phase} $\hat f$ is applied to new examples, and its risk is estimated by
$\ERisk(\hat f, \Dtest)$ on a set never used before. That estimate is honest when $\Dtest$ played no part in
building $\hat f$, and precise when $\Dtest$ is large enough (standard error $\sigma_L/\sqrt{n'}$,
Section~\ref{sec:unbiased}).

\section{Estimating generalisation}\label{sec:estimating}

Throughout this section $A$ denotes a learning algorithm with fixed hyperparameters: given a training set it returns
a predictor. $T_A(n)$ is its training cost on $n$ examples and $c$ the cost of one prediction.

\subsection{Hold-out}

Shuffle the data and split it: train on one part, report the empirical risk on the other
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:holdout}). It is simple and correct; its two weaknesses with little data --- held-out examples are
not used for training, and the standard error $\sigma_L/\sqrt{n'}$ is large --- are compared with the other protocols
in the table at the end of this section. A common compromise holds out 20--30\%.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Hold-out evaluation}
\label{alg:holdout}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $D$ of size $n$, algorithm $A$, held-out fraction $\rho$ (e.g.\ $0.2$), with $\lceil \rho n \rceil \ge 2$
\Ensure estimate $\hat R$ of the risk of $A(\Dtrain)$ and its standard error
\Function{HoldOut}{$D, A, \rho$}
  \State shuffle $D$ \Comment{or split by group or time, Section~\ref{sec:structure}}
  \State $n' \gets \lceil \rho n \rceil$; \ $\Dtest \gets$ first $n'$ examples; \ $\Dtrain \gets$ the rest
  \State $\hat f \gets A(\Dtrain)$
  \For{$j = 1, \dots, n'$} \State $L_j \gets L(\hat f(\x_j), y_j)$ \Comment{$(\x_j, y_j) \in \Dtest$} \EndFor
  \State $\hat R \gets \frac{1}{n'} \sum_j L_j$; \ $s \gets \sqrt{\frac{1}{n'-1}\sum_j (L_j - \hat R)^2}$
  \State \Return $\hat f$, $\hat R$, $s/\sqrt{n'}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}-fold cross-validation}

Split the data into $k$ blocks (folds) of roughly equal size.\footnote{Both uses of the letter are standard: $k$ counts the folds of $k$-fold
cross-validation and the neighbours of $k$-NN. In this note $k$ means folds in Sections~\ref{sec:estimating}
and~\ref{sec:selection}, except in the name ``$k$-NN''; in Section~\ref{sec:capacity} and in questions about $k$-NN
it means neighbours.} For $j = 1, \dots, k$, train on all blocks except $j$
and compute the loss on block $j$; average the results (Algorithm~\ref{alg:cv}, Figure~\ref{fig:splits}, left). Every
example is used once for evaluation and $k - 1$ times for training. Typical values are $k = 5$ or $10$.

Cross-validation estimates the performance of the \emph{procedure} $A$ trained on a fraction $(k-1)/k$ of the data,
not of one particular fitted function: the $k$ fitted predictors are different. Because each is trained on fewer
examples than the final model will be, the estimate is slightly pessimistic; larger $k$ reduces this bias. The $k$
fold errors are not independent (the training sets overlap), so the sample standard deviation of
$e_1, \dots, e_k$ divided by $\sqrt k$ is only a rough standard error, but it is still useful for judging whether two settings differ (the one-standard-error rule, Section~\ref{sec:selection}).

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{$k$-fold cross-validation}
\label{alg:cv}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $D$ of size $n$, algorithm $A$, number of folds $k$
\Ensure cross-validation estimate $\mathrm{CV}$ and the fold errors $e_1, \dots, e_k$
\Function{CrossValidate}{$D, A, k$}
  \State assign each example (or each whole group) at random to one of $k$ folds $B_1, \dots, B_k$ of near-equal size
  \For{$j = 1, \dots, k$}
    \State $\hat f_j \gets A(D \setminus B_j)$ \Comment{train without fold $j$}
    \State $e_j \gets \ERisk(\hat f_j, B_j)$ \Comment{evaluate on fold $j$}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathrm{CV} = \sum_j |B_j|\, e_j / n$ and $(e_1, \dots, e_k)$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Leave-one-out}

The extreme case $k = n$ is \emph{leave-one-out} cross-validation (LOO; the same leave-one-out idea applied to the bias
or variance of a statistic is the jackknife of statistics): each example is predicted
by a model trained on all the others (Algorithm~\ref{alg:loo}). It wastes almost no data and has almost no
pessimistic bias, but it needs $n$ trainings, and its estimate can have a high variance because the $n$ training sets
are nearly identical. Two important cases are cheap. \emph{Memory-based methods} ($k$-NN, Parzen windows, Note~5)
have no training: leave-one-out only means excluding the point itself from its own neighbourhood, one pass over all
pairwise distances, $O(n^2 d)$. \emph{Linear smoothers} --- methods whose predictions are fixed linear combinations of the training targets, such as
the constant predictor and the least-squares and ridge regression of Note~8 --- have an exact
shortcut that obtains all $n$ leave-one-out residuals from a single fit~\cite{hastie2009}; the constant predictor
below is the simplest case.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Leave-one-out cross-validation}
\label{alg:loo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $D = \{(\x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, algorithm $A$
\Ensure leave-one-out estimate $\mathrm{LOO}$
\Function{LeaveOneOut}{$D, A$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $\hat f_{-i} \gets A(D \setminus \{(\x_i, y_i)\})$
    \State $L_i \gets L(\hat f_{-i}(\x_i), y_i)$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathrm{LOO} = \frac1n \sum_i L_i$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{The LOO shortcut for the constant predictor} Removing $y_i$ changes the mean to
$\bar y_{-i} = (n\bar y - y_i)/(n-1)$, so the left-out residual is
\[
  y_i - \bar y_{-i} = \frac{(n-1)y_i - n\bar y + y_i}{n-1} = \frac{n}{n-1}\,(y_i - \bar y) .
\]
Every LOO residual is the ordinary residual inflated by $n/(n-1)$, and
$\mathrm{LOO} = \big(\tfrac{n}{n-1}\big)^2 \times$ training error: one fit instead of $n$.

\begin{worked}{3-fold cross-validation by hand}

\subsection{Splits that respect the structure of the data}\label{sec:structure}

Shuffling assumes i.i.d.\ examples. When that fails (Note~1, Section~2), the split must follow the dependence:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Groups}: all measurements of the same galaxy, patient, sample or detector run go into the same block, or
the model is tested on objects it has already seen.
\item \emph{Time}: for forecasting, train on the past and validate on the block that follows, then roll the window
forward (\emph{sequential validation}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:sequential} and Figure~\ref{fig:splits}, right). A random
split would let the model learn from the future. When hyperparameters are chosen as well, each step has three parts:
choose the setting on block $B_t$ with models trained on the earlier blocks, retrain the winner on everything up to
and including $B_t$, and report its error on $B_{t+1}$. This is Algorithm~\ref{alg:selection} of
Section~\ref{sec:selection} with time-ordered training, validation and test sets, repeated as the origin rolls
forward.
\item \emph{Simulation and reality}: a model trained and validated on simulation must still be tested on real data,
or on a control region of it, before its performance is believed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Sequential (rolling-origin) validation for time-ordered data}
\label{alg:sequential}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require time blocks $B_1, \dots, B_T$, algorithm $A_\gamma$ with candidate settings $\Gamma$ ($G$ of them), initial
blocks $s$, window $w$ ($w = \infty$: expanding)
\Ensure test errors $e_{s+2}, \dots, e_T$ and their mean
\Function{SequentialValidation}{$B_1, \dots, B_T, A, \Gamma, s, w$}
  \For{$t = s+1, \dots, T-1$}
    \State $D_{\mathrm{past}} \gets B_{\max(1,\, t-w)} \cup \dots \cup B_{t-1}$ \Comment{the last $w$ blocks, or all of the past}
    \For{each $\gamma \in \Gamma$}
      \State $v_\gamma \gets \ERisk(A_\gamma(D_{\mathrm{past}}), B_t)$ \Comment{validate on the next block}
    \EndFor
    \State $\gamma_t^* \gets \argmin_\gamma v_\gamma$; \ $\hat f_t \gets A_{\gamma_t^*}(D_{\mathrm{past}} \cup B_t)$ \Comment{retrain, now including $B_t$}
    \State $e_{t+1} \gets \ERisk(\hat f_t, B_{t+1})$ \Comment{test on the block after}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $(e_{s+2}, \dots, e_T)$ and their mean
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
With a single setting ($G = 1$) nothing is chosen, so no validation block is needed: train on the past and evaluate
directly on the block that follows.
\end{algorithm}

Errors $e_t$ that grow with $t$ reveal drift: the process is changing and the model must be retrained regularly. When
neighbouring time points are strongly correlated, leave a gap between the training and validation blocks.

A yearly quantity takes the values $y = 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18$ in years $1$--$6$ (one value per block, so that
everything can be done by hand). Two forecasters compete: M predicts the \emph{mean} of all past years, L the

After each choice L is ``retrained'' on the years up to and including the validation year, i.e.\ it forecasts that

\subsection{Nested cross-validation}

When the same small dataset must both select hyperparameters and estimate the error of the whole procedure, use two
loops --- \emph{nested} or \emph{double cross-validation} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:nested}). We write $\gamma$ for one
hyperparameter setting (a single value or a tuple of values), $A_\gamma$ for the algorithm run with it and $\Gamma$
for the set of candidates; the letter $\lambda$ is kept for a penalty strength (Section~\ref{sec:regularisation}).
The two loops are an outer cross-validation for the estimate and, inside each outer training set, an
inner cross-validation for the selection. The outer test fold never influences any choice, so the outer estimate is
honest for the \emph{procedure} ``tune by inner CV, then train''. The hyperparameters chosen in different outer
folds may differ; that is expected, and their spread is itself informative.


\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Nested cross-validation}
\label{alg:nested}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $D$, algorithm family $A_\gamma$, candidate settings $\Gamma$ ($G$ of them), outer folds $K$, inner folds $k$
\Ensure estimate of the risk of the whole tuning-and-training procedure
\Function{NestedCV}{$D, A, \Gamma, K, k$}
  \State split $D$ at random into outer folds $C_1, \dots, C_K$
  \For{$j = 1, \dots, K$}
    \State $D_j \gets D \setminus C_j$ \Comment{outer training set}
    \For{each $\gamma \in \Gamma$}
      \State $v_\gamma \gets \Call{CrossValidate}{D_j, A_\gamma, k}$ \Comment{inner loop, Algorithm~\ref{alg:cv}}
    \EndFor
    \State $\gamma_j^* \gets \argmin_\gamma v_\gamma$; \ $\hat f_j \gets A_{\gamma_j^*}(D_j)$
    \State $e_j \gets \ERisk(\hat f_j, C_j)$ \Comment{$C_j$ untouched until now}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\frac1K \sum_j e_j$ \Comment{final model: tune by CV on all of $D$, then refit}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{worked}{nested cross-validation on six points}
Take the six points of the 3-fold example above as outer folds ($K = 3$, consecutive pairs). Inside each outer
training set of four points, leave-one-out chooses between the constant predictor and $1$-NN regression (when two
neighbours are equally close, average their targets). For the constant, the LOO shortcut gives the inner score
directly: on the targets $3, 6, 5, 7$ of outer fold~1 the training error is $8.75/4 = 2.1875$, and $(4/3)^2 \times
2.1875 = 3.89$.

\midrule

The outer errors are those of the 3-fold example for the chosen method, so the nested estimate is

\begin{runtime}{evaluation protocols}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.17\linewidth}L{0.21\linewidth}L{0.25\linewidth}L{0.27\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Protocol & Training cost & Use it when & Watch out for \\
\midrule
Hold-out (Alg.~\ref{alg:holdout}) & $T_A(n - n')$, one training & $n$ large; training expensive & noisy if $n'$ is small \\
$k$-fold (Alg.~\ref{alg:cv}) & $k\,T_A\big(\frac{k-1}{k}n\big)$ & $n$ moderate; the default & groups straddling folds \\
Leave-one-out (Alg.~\ref{alg:loo}) & $n\,T_A(n-1)$, or one fit with a shortcut & $n$ small; $k$-NN, Parzen, linear smoothers & high variance; cost without a shortcut \\
Sequential (Alg.~\ref{alg:sequential}) & $(T - s - 1)(G + 1)$ trainings, each at most $T_A(n)$ & time-ordered data & leakage across the time boundary \\
Nested (Alg.~\ref{alg:nested}) & $K(Gk+1)$ trainings, $+\,(Gk+1)$ for the final model & small $n$; honest estimate of a tuned procedure & cost \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\par\smallskip
Here $n$ is the number of examples, $T_A(n)$ the cost of one training, $c$ the cost of one prediction and $G$ the number
of hyperparameter settings. \emph{Prediction}: every protocol predicts each example at most once per training, $O(nc)$ per round. \emph{Memory}:
$O(nd)$ for the data, $O(n)$ for the fold indices and one model at a time; nested CV also keeps $G$ scores per outer
fold. \emph{Why the terms}: $k$-fold trains $k$ times on all but one
fold, so for $T_A$ linear in $n$ it costs about $k - 1$ full trainings; naive leave-one-out of least squares,
$T_A = O(nd^2 + d^3)$, costs $O(n^2d^2 + nd^3)$; sequential validation trains the $G$ candidates and one refit at each of its $T - s - 1$ steps; nested CV runs a full
inner search ($Gk$ trainings) plus one refit
in each of $K$ outer folds --- with $K = k = 5$ and $G = 20$, $505$ trainings, about $8.4$ hours at one minute each,
plus $101$ more to tune and refit the final model on all of $D$.
All folds, steps and settings are independent, so on $N_{\mathrm{proc}}$ processors or GPUs the wall-clock time drops by up to a
factor $N_{\mathrm{proc}}$.
\end{runtime}

Whatever the protocol, the same three rules apply: every choice is made without the data used for the final
estimate; the split follows the dependence structure of the data; and every reported error comes with an error bar.

\section{Capacity, underfitting and overfitting}\label{sec:capacity}

The \emph{capacity} of a family $\F$ is, informally, how many different patterns it can fit. It usually grows with the
number of free parameters, but what matters is the effective number of degrees of freedom: constraints, shared
parameters or regularisation can make a large model behave like a small one. For $k$-NN, which has no fitted
parameters at all, the effective number of parameters is about $n/k$ (roughly one local average per neighbourhood of
$k$ points)~\cite{hastie2009}. For restricted settings capacity can be measured exactly. The
\emph{Vapnik--Chervonenkis (VC) dimension} of a family of classifiers is the largest number $N$ such that \emph{some}
arrangement of $N$ points can be given every one of the $2^N$ labellings by members of the family (it is enough that
one arrangement works: three points on a line cannot be labelled $+,-,+$ by a straight line, but three points in general
position can). For affine classifiers $f(\x) = \ind{\vect{w}\T\x + b > 0}$ in $\R^d$ it is $d + 1$. In the plane
($d = 2$), three points not on a line can be given all $2^3 = 8$ labellings by straight lines, but no four points can.
We only check the square here: for its four corners, the labelling that puts diagonally opposite corners in the same
class is impossible (the general proof for four points is longer).

Figure~\ref{fig:polyfits} shows the three regimes on $15$ noisy points from $\sin(\pi x)$. A straight line cannot follow
the curve (\emph{underfitting}: high training and test error). A polynomial of degree~$12$ passes close to every point and
oscillates wildly between them (\emph{overfitting}: low training error, high test error). Degree~$3$ captures the trend
and ignores the noise.

The same three regimes appear in classification, where capacity shows up as the flexibility of the decision boundary.
Note~5, Section~1, shows $k$-NN boundaries for several $k$ with their training and test errors, and Note~3, Section~2,
shows histogram classifiers with coarser and finer cells. The boundary that generalises best does not have the
fewest training errors (in Note~5, about 6\% for $k = 15$ against 0\% for $k = 1$). It can even make \emph{more}
training errors than an underfitting straight line: what it is judged on is the error on new data, and a boundary that
bends to avoid every training error is following noise.

\paragraph{Validation curves and learning curves} A plot of training and validation error against a hyperparameter
at fixed $n$, such as Figure~\ref{fig:capacity}, is a \emph{validation curve}; some texts also call it a learning curve.
In this course a \emph{learning curve} is the error against the training-set size $n$, as in
Figure~\ref{fig:learningcurve}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:curves} computes both.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Validation curve and learning curve}
\label{alg:curves}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training set $\Dtrain$ of size $n$, validation set $\Dvalid$, algorithm $A_\gamma$
\Function{ValidationCurve}{settings $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_G$}
  \For{$g = 1, \dots, G$}
    \State $\hat f \gets A_{\gamma_g}(\Dtrain)$; \ record $\ERisk(\hat f, \Dtrain)$ and $\ERisk(\hat f, \Dvalid)$
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\Function{LearningCurve}{setting $\gamma$, sizes $n_1 < n_2 < \dots < n_r = n$}
  \State shuffle $\Dtrain$ once \Comment{nested subsets: $D_1 \subset D_2 \subset \dots$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, r$}
    \State $D_i \gets$ the first $n_i$ examples; \ $\hat f \gets A_\gamma(D_i)$
    \State record $\ERisk(\hat f, D_i)$ and $\ERisk(\hat f, \Dvalid)$
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{validation and learning curves}
\emph{Validation curve}: $\sum_g T_{A_{\gamma_g}}(n)$, one training per setting, plus $O(G(n + n_{\mathrm{valid}})c)$
for the evaluations. \emph{Learning curve}: $\sum_i T_A(n_i) \le r\,T_A(n)$; for sizes that double ($n_i = n/2^{r-i}$)
and a training cost linear in $n$ the sum is below $2\,T_A(n)$, about two full trainings. In practice each curve is
averaged over several random splits (Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} uses $200$), which multiplies the cost. \emph{Memory}:
$O(nd)$ for the data and $O(G)$ or $O(r)$ numbers for the curves.
\end{runtime}

\paragraph{Reading a learning curve} Curves of training and validation error against $n$, as in
Figure~\ref{fig:learningcurve}, diagnose what limits a model. If both errors are close to each other but high, the
model underfits: more data will not help, a richer family will. If the training error is low and the validation error
much higher, the model overfits: more data, a smaller family or stronger regularisation will help. The size of the gap
at the current $n$, and how fast it is closing, tells whether collecting more data is worth the cost.

\begin{worked}{counting capacity and reading two learning curves}
\emph{Effective size of $k$-NN.} With $n = 200$ training points, $k$-NN behaves like a model with about $n/k$
parameters: $200$ for $k = 1$ (every point its own region), $40$ for $k = 5$, $10$ for $k = 20$ and $1$ for $k = 200$,
where every query receives the same average --- the constant predictor of Section~\ref{sec:ingredients}. Increasing
$k$ therefore \emph{lowers} the capacity, although nothing is ``removed'' from the model.

\emph{Two diagnoses.} At $n = 1000$, model~A has training error $0.30$ and validation error $0.32$, and both curves
have been flat since $n = 300$. Model~B has training error $0.02$ and validation error $0.25$, and the gap shrinks
slowly as $n$ grows. A underfits: the gap ($0.02$) is small, so more data cannot help; a richer family or better
features can. B overfits: the gap ($0.23$) dominates, so more data, a smaller family or stronger regularisation will
help. Both curves are useful only because the validation errors are computed on data the models never saw.
\end{worked}

\section{Where the error comes from}\label{sec:decomposition}

\subsection{Approximation and estimation error}

Let $f^* = \argmin_f \Risk(f)$ be the best possible predictor (its risk is the Bayes risk of Note~1, Section~4),
$f_{\F} = \argmin_{f \in \F} \Risk(f)$ the best member of the family, and $\hat f$ the function returned by the
algorithm. Adding and subtracting $\Risk(f_{\F})$,
\[
  \Risk(\hat f) - \Risk(f^*) = \underbrace{\Risk(f_{\F}) - \Risk(f^*)}_{\text{approximation error}}
  + \underbrace{\Risk(\hat f) - \Risk(f_{\F})}_{\text{estimation error}} .
\]
Both terms are non-negative: $f^*$ is the best of all functions, and $f_{\F}$ is the best in $\F$, which contains
$\hat f$. The approximation error depends only on the family and the problem; it shrinks (or stays the same) as $\F$
grows. The estimation error --- the price of learning $f_{\F}$ from finitely many noisy examples --- is random,
usually grows with the size of $\F$ at fixed $n$, and shrinks with $n$.

\begin{worked}{approximating a parabola with constants, lines and parabolas}
Let $X$ be uniform on $[-1, 1]$ and $Y = X^2 + \varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2 = 0.04$, squared loss. The
Bayes predictor is $f^*(x) = x^2$ with risk $0.04$, and $\Risk(f) - \Risk(f^*) = \E[(f(X) - X^2)^2]$ (Note~1,
Section~4).

\emph{Constants.} $\E[(b - X^2)^2]$ is minimised by $b = \E[X^2] = \frac12\int_{-1}^{1} x^2\,dx = \frac13$, and
with $\E[X^4] = \frac12\int_{-1}^{1} x^4\,dx = \frac15$ the approximation error is
$\Var(X^2) = \E[X^4] - (\E X^2)^2 = \frac15 - \frac19 = \frac{4}{45} = 0.089$. For the sample mean the estimation
error is $\E[(\bar y - \frac13)^2] = \Var(Y)/n$, and $\Var(Y) = \Var(X^2) + \Var(\varepsilon) = 0.089 + 0.04$
because $X$ and $\varepsilon$ are independent.

\emph{Lines.} Minimise $\E[(a + bX - X^2)^2]$ over $a$ and $b$. Setting the two partial derivatives to zero,
\[
  \E[a + bX - X^2] = 0, \qquad \E[(a + bX - X^2)X] = 0,
\]
gives $a = \E[X^2] - b\,\E[X]$ and, after substituting, $b = \Cov(X, X^2)/\Var(X)$. Here $\E[X] = 0$ and
$\Cov(X, X^2) = \E[X^3] - \E[X]\E[X^2] = 0$ by symmetry, so $b = 0$ and $a = \frac13$: the best line is the same
constant, and the approximation error is \emph{unchanged}, $0.089$. (Without any algebra: $X^2$ is even in $X$, so a
slope can only add error.) The extra parameter adds only estimation error.

\emph{Parabolas.} $f^*$ is in the family: approximation error $0$.

Estimation errors from $10^5$ simulated training sets (least-squares fits):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
 & approximation & estimation & excess & estimation & excess \\
 & & $n = 10$ & $n = 10$ & $n = 100$ & $n = 100$ \\
\midrule
constants & 0.089 & 0.013 & 0.102 & 0.0013 & 0.090 \\
lines & 0.089 & 0.047 & 0.136 & 0.0032 & 0.092 \\
parabolas & 0 & $\approx 0.03$ & $\approx 0.03$ & 0.0012 & 0.0012 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The ``excess'' columns are $\Risk(\hat f) - \Risk(f^*)$, approximation plus estimation; add the noise $0.04$ for the
expected risk itself. A bigger family is not automatically better: lines are worse than constants here. Parabolas, on
the other hand, have a \emph{smaller} estimation error than lines although they have more parameters: for lines the
unexplained curvature acts like extra noise (residual variance $0.089 + 0.04 = 0.129$ instead of $0.04$), and for
large $n$ the estimation error is roughly residual variance $\times$ number of parameters $/\,n$ --- at $n = 100$,
$0.129 \times 2/100 = 0.0026$ for lines and $0.04 \times 3/100 = 0.0012$ for parabolas. With the right family the
estimation error dominates, and it falls roughly like $1/n$.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Bias and variance for squared loss}

Let $y = f^*(\x) + \varepsilon$ with $\E[\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\Var(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, and let $\hat f_D$ be
trained on a random training set $D$. At a fixed $\x$, averaging over both $D$ and the noise of a new observation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biasvar}
  \E\big[(y - \hat f_D(\x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}
  + \underbrace{\big(f^*(\x) - \E_D[\hat f_D(\x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
  + \underbrace{\Var_D\big(\hat f_D(\x)\big)}_{\text{variance}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Derivation} Fix $\x$ and abbreviate $F = \hat f_D(\x)$ (random through $D$), $\bar F = \E_D[F]$ and
$f^* = f^*(\x)$. The new noise $\varepsilon$ is independent of $D$.

\emph{Step 1: separate the noise.} Expanding the square,
\[
  \E\big[(f^* + \varepsilon - F)^2\big] = \E[\varepsilon^2] + 2\,\E\big[\varepsilon\,(f^* - F)\big]
  + \E\big[(f^* - F)^2\big] .
\]
By independence $\E[\varepsilon (f^* - F)] = \E[\varepsilon]\,\E[f^* - F] = 0$, and $\E[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2$.
So the expected error is $\sigma^2 + \E[(f^* - F)^2]$, which is Note~1's decomposition with the trained model in
place of a fixed one.

\emph{Step 2: separate bias and variance.} Add and subtract $\bar F$:
\[
  \E\big[(f^* - F)^2\big] = \E\big[(f^* - \bar F + \bar F - F)^2\big]
  = (f^* - \bar F)^2 + 2\,(f^* - \bar F)\,\E[\bar F - F] + \E\big[(\bar F - F)^2\big] .
\]
The first term is a constant (it does not depend on $D$). The middle term vanishes because $\E[\bar F - F] = \bar F -
\bar F = 0$. The last term is $\Var_D(F)$ by definition. Combining the two steps gives \eqref{eq:biasvar}; averaging
it over $\x$ gives the same decomposition of the risk.

\paragraph{Interpretation} Bias measures how far the \emph{average} fitted function is from the truth; variance
measures how much the fit changes from one training set to another. A richer family lowers bias and raises variance;
more data lowers variance (Figure~\ref{fig:learningcurve}), which is why collecting more data is often the most
effective way to reduce overfitting.
Figure~\ref{fig:biasvar} computes both terms exactly for polynomial fits to $15$ equally spaced points with noise
$\sigma = 0.3$: degree~1 has squared bias $0.209$ and variance $0.011$; degree~3 balances them ($0.006$ and $0.021$,
total expected error $0.116$ including the noise $0.09$); degree~12 has negligible bias and variance $0.486$. Bias
roughly corresponds to approximation error and variance to estimation error, although the two decompositions are not
identical.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/n4_biasvar.pdf}
\caption{Bias and variance. Left and middle: 20 least-squares fits, each to a fresh noisy sample at the same 15 inputs
(dashed: the truth). Right: squared bias and variance, averaged over $x \in [-1, 1]$, and their sum plus the noise.}
\label{fig:biasvar}
\end{figure}

\begin{worked}{a biased estimator that beats the unbiased one}
Estimate a mean $\mu$ from $n$ observations with variance $\sigma^2$ by the \emph{shrunk} mean $\hat\mu_a = a\,\bar y$,
$0 \le a \le 1$. Since $\E[\bar y] = \mu$ and $\Var(\bar y) = \sigma^2/n$,
\[
  \text{bias} = \E[\hat\mu_a] - \mu = (a - 1)\mu, \qquad \text{variance} = a^2\,\sigma^2/n .
\]
The mean squared error of the estimator, $\E[(\hat\mu_a - \mu)^2]$, splits exactly as in Step~2 of the derivation
above, with $\mu$ in place of $f^*$ and $\hat\mu_a$ in place of $F$: it is bias$^2$ $+$ variance. There is no noise
term, because the estimate is compared with $\mu$ itself, not with a new noisy observation.
Take $\mu = 2$, $\sigma^2 = 4$, $n = 4$, so $\sigma^2/n = 1$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
$a$ & 1 & 0.9 & 0.8 & 0.7 & 0.5 & 0 \\
\midrule
bias$^2 = (1-a)^2 \mu^2$ & 0 & 0.04 & 0.16 & 0.36 & 1.00 & 4.00 \\
variance $= a^2 \sigma^2/n$ & 1.00 & 0.81 & 0.64 & 0.49 & 0.25 & 0 \\
mean squared error & 1.00 & 0.85 & \textbf{0.80} & 0.85 & 1.25 & 4.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Setting the derivative of $(1 - a)^2\mu^2 + a^2\sigma^2/n$ to zero, $-2(1-a)\mu^2 + 2a\sigma^2/n = 0$, gives
$a^* = \mu^2/(\mu^2 + \sigma^2/n) = 4/5$: shrinking by 20\% lowers the error from $1.00$ to $0.80$ (a simulation gives
$0.996$ and $0.795$). For predicting a new observation the noise $\sigma^2 = 4$ is added to every column. The catch is
that $a^*$ depends on the unknown $\mu$; in practice the amount of shrinkage is a hyperparameter chosen on validation
data. In fact $a\,\bar y$ is the minimiser of the regularised objective $\frac1n\sum_i (y_i - b)^2 + \lambda b^2$ with
$a = 1/(1 + \lambda)$ (Question~B6), the one-parameter version of ridge regression (Note~8, Section~4).
\end{worked}

\section{Model selection}\label{sec:selection}

\paragraph{Parameters and hyperparameters} \emph{Parameters} are what the training algorithm fits.
\emph{Hyperparameters} are set before training starts: a polynomial degree or a network architecture selects the
family, a penalty strength $\lambda$ says which of its members are preferred, a learning rate or a number of epochs
steers the optimiser. As in Section~\ref{sec:estimating}, $\gamma$ denotes one complete setting of the
hyperparameters and $\lambda$ only a penalty strength. Most of them control capacity, which is why they cannot be chosen on the training set: the training
error would always favour the largest capacity.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.25\linewidth}L{0.37\linewidth}L{0.30\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Method & Hyperparameters & Parameters \\
\midrule
Histogram (Note~3) & number and placement of bins & counts in each cell \\
Decision tree (Note~3) & maximum depth, minimum leaf size & split variables, thresholds, leaf values \\
$k$-NN (Note~5) & $k$, the distance & the stored training set \\
Parzen windows (Note~5) & kernel and its width & the stored training set \\
Gaussian Bayes classifier (Note~6) & shared or separate covariances, shrinkage & class means, covariances, priors \\
Polynomial, ridge, lasso regression (Note~8) & degree, $\lambda$, whether and how to scale features & coefficients \\
Logistic regression, SVM (Note~9) & $\lambda$ or $C$; kernel and width (Note~10) & weights and bias \\
Neural network & architecture, learning rate, batch size, training time, $\lambda$ & weights and biases \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{worked}{sorting the choices of a real pipeline}
A $5$-NN classifier with Euclidean distance is trained on standardised features, using 20\% of the data for
validation.
\begin{itemize}
\item $k = 5$ and the Euclidean distance: \emph{hyperparameters}, chosen on validation data.
\item Whether to standardise at all: a \emph{hyperparameter}. The means and standard deviations it uses:
\emph{parameters} --- they are computed from data, so
they must be computed from the training part only (Note~1, Section~5). Computing them on all data is leakage: the
validation examples then shape the features the model sees, which tends to make the estimate optimistic (usually only
slightly for standardisation, much more for steps such as feature selection).
\item The stored training examples: the parameters of a memory-based method.
\item The 20\% validation fraction and the random seed of the split: \emph{neither}; they belong to the evaluation
protocol. The seed should not be tuned, and the variation across seeds is part of the error bar.
\item The decision threshold of a probabilistic classifier: a hyperparameter if chosen to optimise a metric on
validation data, a fixed rule ($\frac12$) otherwise.
\end{itemize}
\end{worked}

\subsection{Selection with a training, a validation and a test set}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:selection} is the basic meta-algorithm of the course: every candidate is trained on $\Dtrain$,
scored on $\Dvalid$, and only the winner is evaluated, once, on $\Dtest$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{Model selection with a training, a validation and a test set}
\label{alg:selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $D$, candidate algorithms $A$, each with a set $\Gamma_A$ of hyperparameter settings
\Ensure selected model $\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}}$ and an honest estimate of its risk
\Function{SelectModel}{$D$, $\{(A, \Gamma_A)\}$}
\State Shuffle (or split by group or time, Section~\ref{sec:structure}) and divide $D$ into $\Dtrain$, $\Dvalid$, $\Dtest$
\For{each candidate algorithm $A$ and each setting $\gamma \in \Gamma_A$}
  \State $\hat f_{A,\gamma} \gets A_\gamma(\Dtrain)$ \Comment{fit parameters}
  \State $e_{A,\gamma} \gets \ERisk(\hat f_{A,\gamma}, \Dvalid)$ \Comment{out-of-sample score}
\EndFor
\State $(A^*, \gamma^*) \gets \argmin e_{A,\gamma}$; \ $\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}} \gets \hat f_{A^*,\gamma^*}$
\State Optionally refit: $\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}} \gets A^*_{\gamma^*}(\Dtrain \cup \Dvalid)$
\State \Return $\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}}$ and $\ERisk(\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}}, \Dtest)$ \Comment{$\Dtest$ used once, at the very end}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{model selection with train/validation/test}
With $G$ candidates (algorithms times settings): $\sum_{A,\gamma} T_{A_\gamma}(n_{\mathrm{train}})$ for training,
$O(G\,n_{\mathrm{valid}}\,c)$ for validation, one optional refit and $O(n_{\mathrm{test}}\,c)$ for the final test.
Replacing the validation set by $k$-fold cross-validation multiplies the training cost by about $k$. Prediction: the
selected model costs $c$ per query; the selection adds nothing at prediction time. Memory: the data, $O(nd)$, plus
the best model so far (or none, refitting at the end).
\end{runtime}

The validation error of the winner is itself optimistic --- it is the minimum over many noisy estimates --- which is
why a separate test set is needed. The effect is easy to quantify. Suppose $20$ settings are tried on $200$
validation examples of a balanced binary problem, and \emph{none} of them is better than guessing: each has true error
$50\%$ and, by \eqref{eq:se}, a standard error of $3.5$ points. The smallest of the $20$ validation errors is then on
average $43.4\%$ (by simulation, assuming the settings err independently of one another; correlated settings give a
smaller but still positive optimism), an apparent improvement of more than six points that does not exist; with $200$
settings the best reaches $40.3\%$. Cross-validation can replace the single validation set in line~5, which reduces
the noise but does not remove this selection effect.

\paragraph{The one-standard-error rule} Validation curves are often flat near their minimum, and the differences
between the best few settings are within noise. A common, conservative choice is to take the \emph{simplest} model
(lowest capacity, strongest regularisation) whose validation error is within one standard error of the
best~\cite{hastie2009} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:onese}). It needs no training beyond the cross-validation that produced the
errors: $O(G)$ comparisons.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\small
\caption{The one-standard-error rule}
\label{alg:onese}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require settings $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_G$ ordered from simplest to most complex, their cross-validation errors
$\mathrm{CV}_g$ and standard errors $\mathrm{SE}_g$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:cv})
\Function{OneSERule}{$\mathrm{CV}_{1..G}, \mathrm{SE}_{1..G}$}
  \State $g^* \gets \argmin_g \mathrm{CV}_g$ \Comment{the best setting}
  \State \Return the smallest $g$ with $\mathrm{CV}_g \le \mathrm{CV}_{g^*} + \mathrm{SE}_{g^*}$ \Comment{simplest within one SE}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{worked}{choosing a polynomial degree with the one-standard-error rule}
Ten-fold cross-validation on the training and validation data gives, for degrees $1$--$6$ (mean $\pm$ standard error of
the fold errors):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
degree & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\
\midrule
$\mathrm{CV} \pm \mathrm{SE}$ & $0.42 \pm 0.03$ & $0.15 \pm 0.02$ & $0.110 \pm 0.015$ & $0.105 \pm 0.015$ & $0.108 \pm 0.02$ & $0.12 \pm 0.025$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The minimum is at degree~$4$, $0.105$, so the threshold is $0.105 + 0.015 = 0.120$. Degree~$2$ ($0.15$) is above it,
degree~$3$ ($0.110$) below: the rule picks degree~$3$. Degrees $3$--$6$ are indistinguishable within their error bars,
and the simplest of them has the lowest variance. Refit degree~$3$ on all training and validation data and evaluate it
once on the test set; that single number is what is reported.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Searching the hyperparameter space}\label{sec:search}

With one hyperparameter one evaluates a list of values, usually on a logarithmic scale for scale-like quantities such
as $\lambda$, a kernel width or a learning rate ($10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 1$). With several hyperparameters there are
two standard strategies (Algorithm~\ref{alg:search}). \emph{Grid search} evaluates every combination of a few values
per hyperparameter. \emph{Random search} draws each setting at random from a chosen distribution and stops when the
budget is spent.

\begin{algorithm}[H]\small
\caption{Grid search and random search}
\label{alg:search}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a scoring routine $\mathrm{Score}(\gamma)$ (validation error, or \Call{CrossValidate}{} of $A_\gamma$); $h$ hyperparameters
\Function{GridSearch}{value lists $V_1, \dots, V_h$}
  \For{each $\gamma \in V_1 \times V_2 \times \dots \times V_h$} \State $e_\gamma \gets \mathrm{Score}(\gamma)$ \EndFor
  \State \Return $\argmin_\gamma e_\gamma$ \Comment{$|V_1| \cdots |V_h|$ evaluations}
\EndFunction
\Function{RandomSearch}{distributions $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_h$, budget $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$}
  \For{$b = 1, \dots, N_{\mathrm{tr}}$}
    \State draw $\gamma_b = (\gamma_{b1}, \dots, \gamma_{bh})$ with $\gamma_{bj} \sim \pi_j$ independently
    \Statex \Comment{e.g.\ log-uniform for a penalty $\lambda$ and learning rates}
    \State $e_b \gets \mathrm{Score}(\gamma_b)$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\gamma_{b^*}$ with $b^* = \argmin_b e_b$ \Comment{$N_{\mathrm{tr}}$ evaluations, $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$ chosen freely}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{grid search and random search}
Each evaluation costs one training plus validation, or about $k$ trainings with cross-validation. Grid search with $v$
values per hyperparameter needs $v^h$ evaluations: exponential in the number of hyperparameters ($5$ values for $4$
hyperparameters is already $625$ trainings). Random search needs exactly the budget $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$, whatever $h$. Both are
embarrassingly parallel. Prediction: $c$ per query for the selected model; the search adds nothing. Memory: the
list of scores, $O(v^h)$ or $O(N_{\mathrm{tr}})$.
\end{runtime}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/n4_search.pdf}
\caption{Nine trials when only the horizontal hyperparameter matters. Top: the trials in the plane of the two
hyperparameters. Bottom: the validation error, which depends on the important hyperparameter only. The grid tests
just three of its values and misses the dip (best error $0.223$); random search tests nine and finds it ($0.109$).
After the illustration of Bergstra and Bengio~\cite{bergstra2012}.}
\label{fig:search}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why random search usually wins} Usually only a few hyperparameters matter much~\cite{bergstra2012}. A
$3 \times 3$ grid spends nine trainings on only three values of the important hyperparameter, because each value is
repeated for every value of the unimportant one (Figure~\ref{fig:search}). Nine random trials give nine different
values of every hyperparameter. A simple calculation shows how many trials are enough. If a setting counts as
``good'' when it lies in the best 5\% of the search space (in volume), each random trial misses with probability
$0.95$, and $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$ independent trials all miss with probability $0.95^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}$. So $P(\text{at least one good trial}) =
1 - 0.95^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}$: $0.37$ for $N_{\mathrm{tr}} = 9$, $0.64$ for $N_{\mathrm{tr}} = 20$ and $0.95$ for $N_{\mathrm{tr}} = 60$, independently of the number of
hyperparameters. Grids remain fine for one or two hyperparameters, and as a final refinement around a good region.
More elaborate methods (Bayesian optimisation, early termination of bad runs) build on the same scoring routine.

\subsection{Regularisation}\label{sec:regularisation}

Instead of choosing among nested families, one can keep a rich family and add a preference for simple members,
\[
  \hat f_\lambda = \argmin_{f \in \F} \; \ERisk(f, \Dtrain) + \lambda\, \Omega(f),
\]
where $\Omega$ penalises complexity and $\lambda \ge 0$ is a hyperparameter. Weight decay ($\Omega = \norm{\vect{w}}^2$)
is the most common choice; it is studied for linear models as ridge regression (Note~8, Section~4), and the
$\ell_1$ penalty of the lasso (Note~8, Section~5) prefers sparse solutions. Stopping the optimisation early (Note~7,
Section~7), injecting noise (dropout) and augmenting the data with transformed copies act as implicit regularisers.
Shrinking the covariance estimate (Note~2, Section~6) is the same idea. $\Omega$ can even be the degree of a
polynomial: minimising $\ERisk + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{degree}$ trades training error against degree, the soft analogue of choosing the
degree on validation data. Penalised criteria of this kind are known as \emph{complexity penalties}.

\begin{worked}{the value of $\lambda$ decides between two fits}
Four points $(x, y)$: $(-1, -2)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, $(2, 1)$, squared loss, and $\Omega$ the sum of squared
coefficients \emph{excluding} the intercept. Compare two candidates:
\begin{itemize}
\item A, the parabola $1 + 2x - x^2$, which passes through all four points: $\ERisk = 0$, $\Omega = 2^2 + 1^2 = 5$;
\item B, the least-squares line $y = x$, with residuals $-1, 1, 1, -1$: $\ERisk = 1$, $\Omega = 1^2 = 1$.
\end{itemize}
The regularised objectives are $J_A(\lambda) = 0 + 5\lambda$ and $J_B(\lambda) = 1 + \lambda$. For $\lambda = 0.1$:
$J_A = 0.5 < J_B = 1.1$, the interpolating parabola wins. For $\lambda = 0.5$: $J_A = 2.5 > J_B = 1.5$, the line wins.
The preference switches at $5\lambda = 1 + \lambda$, i.e.\ $\lambda = 0.25$. The actual minimiser of $J$ over all
quadratics is neither A nor B (it is the ridge solution of Note~8, Section~4): it moves continuously from the interpolating parabola ($\lambda \to 0$) towards a flatter
curve as $\lambda$ grows, and reaches $J = 0.40$ at $\lambda = 0.1$ and $J = 1.13$ at $\lambda = 0.5$, below both
candidates. Which choice generalises better is not
decided by these four points; $\lambda$ is chosen on validation data. Because $\Omega$ adds coefficients of terms of
different units ($x$, $x^2$), its value depends on how $x$ is scaled --- one reason to standardise features before
regularising (Note~8). With the degree penalty instead, $J_A = 2\lambda$ and $J_B = 1 + \lambda$, and the preference
switches at $\lambda = 1$.
\end{worked}

\begin{physics}{blind analysis}
Particle and nuclear physicists guard against tuning an analysis towards the answer they expect by \emph{blinding}:
the signal region, or the final numerical result, stays hidden while selection criteria and corrections are fixed
on simulation and control regions, and is revealed only once~\cite{klein2005}. This is Algorithm~\ref{alg:selection}
with the test set locked in a drawer --- the discipline that keeps $\ERisk(\hat f_{\mathrm{sel}}, \Dtest)$ honest.
\end{physics}

\begin{modern}{how far does a benchmark number travel}
Benchmark test sets reused by thousands of papers could slowly turn into validation sets.
When a new ImageNet evaluation set was collected by the original procedure, accuracies fell by 11--14 percentage
points, though the ranking of models was largely preserved; the authors traced the drop mainly to small differences
between the old and new data, not to overfitting by reuse~\cite{recht2019}. A test number holds for the distribution
its examples came from. For language models trained on much of the public web a further danger is
\emph{contamination}: benchmark questions and answers may be in the training data. Fresh, private or time-stamped test
sets guard against both.
\end{modern}

\section{Beyond the U-shaped curve}\label{sec:modern}

Modern neural networks often have far more parameters than training examples and can fit even randomly labelled
data~\cite{zhang2017}, yet they generalise well. Figure~\ref{fig:doubledescent} shows a simple model with the same behaviour: least
squares on $p$ random nonlinear features of $n = 100$ training points. Each feature is a fixed random function
$\phi_j(\x) = \max(0, \vect{v}_j\T\x)$, with the direction $\vect{v}_j$ drawn once at random and never trained (a
ReLU feature), and the model is $f(\x) = \sum_{j=1}^p w_j \phi_j(\x)$, linear in the weights $\vect{w}$. As $p$ grows the test error first follows the
classical U, then rises sharply as $p$ approaches $n$ --- where the model can just interpolate the data and the fit is
extremely sensitive to noise --- and then \emph{decreases again}. Beyond $p = n$ there are infinitely many interpolating
solutions, and the algorithm returns the one whose weight vector has the smallest norm $\norm{\vect{w}}$; as $p$ grows, that implicit preference makes the fit
smoother. In this example the test error at $p = 6000$ (0.134) is below the best value of the classical regime
(0.141).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{figures/double_descent.pdf}
\caption{Double descent: median test error of minimum-norm least squares on $p$ random ReLU features of
10-dimensional inputs, $n = 100$ training points, 20 repetitions.}
\label{fig:doubledescent}
\end{figure}

This \emph{double descent}~\cite{belkin2019,nakkiran2020} does not overturn the ideas of this note; it refines what
``capacity'' means. The number of parameters is a poor measure of it; the norm of the solution, set implicitly by the
optimiser, matters more. Validation still decides. The other modern lesson concerns scale: for language models, test
loss falls as a smooth power law in model size, data and compute over many orders of
magnitude~\cite{kaplan2020}, and for a fixed compute budget model size and number of training tokens should grow
together, at roughly twenty tokens per parameter~\cite{hoffmann2022}. Learning curves like
Figure~\ref{fig:learningcurve}, measured on small models, are now used to plan the training of large ones.

\begin{keyidea}[Summary]
A learning algorithm is a family, a loss and an optimiser; the loss decides what ``best'' means, and continuous,
convex surrogates that upper-bound the 0--1 error and have informative slopes make training possible; precision and
recall describe rare classes better than the error rate. On data never used for any choice, the empirical risk is an
unbiased estimate of the expected risk, with standard error $\sigma_L/\sqrt{n'}$ ($\sqrt{p(1-p)/n'}$ for an error rate);
models are best compared pairwise on the same test set. Training minimises empirical risk, which underestimates the
expected risk of the result. Honest estimates come from hold-out, $k$-fold, leave-one-out, sequential or nested
validation, with splits that respect groups and time. Capacity trades approximation against estimation error, and
bias against variance; hyperparameters and regularisation control it and are chosen on validation data --- by random
rather than grid search when there are several --- with the test set used once. Over-parameterised models add a
second descent of the test error, but not a new principle.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{questions}
\begin{conceptual}
\item Name the three ingredients (family, loss, optimiser) of (a) the constant predictor trained with squared loss,
(b) least-squares line fitting, (c) a 1-NN classifier (hint: 1-NN only stores the data; ask what training error it
achieves and what, if anything, is optimised). For which of them is the ``optimiser'' trivial, and why?
\item Why do we not train classifiers by minimising the 0--1 error directly? State two properties a good surrogate
loss should have, and explain why the hinge loss and the cross-entropy in bits have them.
\item Two classifiers have the same test error but different test cross-entropies. What does the difference tell
you? Which one would you prefer for a trigger whose threshold will be tuned later, and why?
\item True or false, with a one-line justification each: (a) the error of a fixed classifier on a fresh test set is an
unbiased estimate of its expected error; (b) the training error of a trained classifier is an unbiased estimate of
its expected error; (c) quadrupling the test set halves the standard error of the test error.
\item Two classifiers are evaluated on the same test set. Why does comparing them example by example detect smaller
differences than comparing their two error rates as if they were independent? Which test examples carry no
information about the difference?
\item Explain in words why the training error of a fitted model is optimistic. Use the constant predictor (the
sample mean) as your example: why is its training error smaller on average than its error on a new point?
\item Explain why choosing $k$ for $k$-NN by minimising the training error always favours $k = 1$ (it attains the
smallest possible training error), and what the training error of $1$-NN is (assume no two training points share the
same input).
\item Compare hold-out evaluation and $k$-fold cross-validation: what does each cost, what does each waste, and when
is hold-out perfectly adequate?
\item Cross-validation is said to estimate the performance of a \emph{procedure}, not of a fitted function. Explain.
Why is the $k$-fold estimate slightly pessimistic, and how does this change with $k$?
\item Galaxy images come from 300 telescope pointings, several galaxies per pointing. Design the cross-validation
splits, and explain what a naive random split would overestimate.
\item You forecast the daily output of a solar plant. Why is a random $k$-fold split wrong here? Describe sequential
validation and say what a growing validation error along the steps would indicate.
\item A data set is standardised (mean and standard deviation computed on all of it) before 5-fold cross-validation.
Why is this a leak, in which direction does it usually bias the estimate, and how should the pipeline be organised?
\item Why does model selection need a validation set \emph{and} a test set? What goes wrong when many settings are
compared on one validation set and the best validation error is reported as the final result?
\item Model~A has training error $0.30$ and validation error $0.32$, both flat as $n$ grows; model~B has $0.02$ and
$0.25$, with the gap closing slowly. Diagnose each, and say whether more data, a richer family or more regularisation
would help.
\item Why can straight lines in the plane not separate every labelling of the four corners of a square, although they
can for any three points not on a line? What does this say about the capacity of linear classifiers in two dimensions?
\item What happens to the bias and to the variance of a model when (a) the polynomial degree increases, (b) the
number of training examples increases, (c) the regularisation strength $\lambda$ increases? Explain each in a
sentence.
\item Define approximation error and estimation error. In the parabola example of Section~\ref{sec:decomposition},
adding a slope to the constant predictor made things worse. Which term grew, and why did the other not shrink?
\item Classify each item as parameter, hyperparameter or neither: the weights of a logistic regression; the
regularisation constant $C$ of an SVM; the bin width of a histogram; the counts in its bins; the random seed of the
train/test split; the means used to standardise features; the number of training epochs of a neural network.
\item Explain with a picture why random search usually finds better settings than grid search for the same number
of trials. When is a grid still a good choice?
\item State the one-standard-error rule. Why does it prefer the simpler model, and why is that reasonable when the
validation curve is flat near its minimum?
\item What problem does nested cross-validation solve, and what does it cost? Why may the outer folds select
different hyperparameters, and why is that not a problem?
\item Does double descent contradict the bias--variance trade-off? Explain what ``capacity'' means for a
minimum-norm interpolating model, and why validation data still decide.
\item A selection keeps 97\% of events correctly classified on a sample in which only 6\% of the events are signal.
Why can this accuracy be misleading? Which two numbers would you report instead, and how are they related to the
signal efficiency and the purity of the selected sample?
\item In sequential validation with hyperparameter selection, why is the setting chosen on block $B_t$ but the error
reported on $B_{t+1}$? What would go wrong if the error on $B_t$ itself were reported?
\end{conceptual}

\begin{calculations}
\item A classifier outputs $g = 0.8, 0.4, 0.1, 0.9$ for four examples with labels $y = 1, 1, 0, 0$. Compute the
empirical risk under the 0--1 loss (threshold $\frac12$), the squared loss $(g - y)^2$ and the cross-entropy (natural
logarithm). Which example contributes most to the squared loss and to the cross-entropy? Why does the 0--1 loss not
distinguish examples~2 and~4?
\item A classifier has 8.0\% error on a test set of 500 examples and another has 7.0\%. Estimate the standard error of
each. Are the two classifiers distinguishable if their errors are treated as independent? On the same test set, the
first is wrong and the second right on 25 examples, and the reverse on 20. Compute the paired standard error of the
difference. For an error rate near 10\%, how large a test set makes two standard errors of \emph{one} classifier's
error at most one percentage point? How large must two independent test sets be to resolve a one-point difference
between two classifiers at two standard errors?
\item Six examples $(x, y)$: $(1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2), (4, 5), (5, 4), (6, 6)$. Compute by hand the 3-fold
cross-validation error (squared loss, folds $\{1, 2\}$, $\{3, 4\}$, $\{5, 6\}$ by $x$) of the constant predictor and of
$1$-NN regression. Which would you choose?
\item For the targets $y = 0, 1, 1, 4, 9$ find the best constant under squared loss and under absolute loss, using the
derivative of the training objective (for the absolute loss, the sign of $J'(b)$ between data points, as in
Section~\ref{sec:ingredients}), and compute both training risks for both constants.
\item For the shrunk mean $a\,\bar y$ with $\mu = 1$, $\sigma^2 = 2$, $n = 2$, compute the squared bias, the variance
and the mean squared error for $a = 1$ and $a = 0.5$. Find the optimal $a$ by setting a derivative to zero.
\item Show by differentiation that $J(b) = \frac1n \sum_i (y_i - b)^2 + \lambda b^2$ is minimised by
$b = \bar y/(1 + \lambda)$. Then, for the one-parameter model $f(x) = wx$ with
$J(w) = \frac1n \sum_i (w x_i - y_i)^2 + \lambda w^2$, show that $w = \sum_i x_i y_i / (\sum_i x_i^2 + n\lambda)$ and
evaluate it for the data $(1, 1), (2, 3)$ with $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 0.5$.
\item Nested cross-validation uses $K = 10$ outer folds, $k = 5$ inner folds and a grid of $4 \times 5$ settings; one
training takes three minutes. How many trainings does the outer estimate need, how many more are needed to tune and
refit the final model on all the data, and how long does everything take on one machine?
\item With random search, how many trials are needed to have at least a 90\% chance that one trial lands in the best
5\% of the search space? Does the answer depend on the number of hyperparameters?
\item On $2000$ test events a classifier gives $\mathrm{TP} = 90$, $\mathrm{FP} = 30$, $\mathrm{FN} = 60$,
$\mathrm{TN} = 1820$. Compute its accuracy, precision, recall and $F_1$. What accuracy does the rule ``always predict
$0$'' reach on the same events?
\item Five regularisation strengths, ordered from the strongest (simplest model) to the weakest, have cross-validation
errors $0.30 \pm 0.02$, $0.22 \pm 0.02$, $0.20 \pm 0.01$, $0.19 \pm 0.015$ and $0.21 \pm 0.02$. Which setting has the
smallest error, and which does the one-standard-error rule choose? Explain the difference in one sentence.
\end{calculations}

\begin{derivations}
\item Prove the bias--variance decomposition \eqref{eq:biasvar}, justifying why each cross term vanishes. Apply it to
$k$-NN regression at a fixed $\x$ whose $k$ neighbours (with fixed inputs) have independent noisy targets
$y_{(j)} = f^*(\x_{(j)}) + \varepsilon_j$: show that the variance is $\sigma^2/k$ and write the squared bias.
\item For the constant predictor fitted to $n$ i.i.d.\ targets, show that the expected training error is
$(1 - 1/n)\sigma^2$ and the expected error on a new target is $(1 + 1/n)\sigma^2$. Then derive the leave-one-out
identity $y_i - \bar y_{-i} = \frac{n}{n-1}(y_i - \bar y)$ and show that the expected LOO error equals
$(1 + \frac{1}{n-1})\sigma^2$, the expected risk of a model trained on $n - 1$ points.
\item Prove that $\E[\ERisk(\hat f, \Dtrain)] \le \Risk(f_{\F}) \le \E[\Risk(\hat f)]$ for empirical risk minimisation
over any family $\F$, and explain where the proof uses that $f_{\F}$ does not depend on the training set.
\item For two models evaluated on the same test set of size $n'$, show that
$\Var(\ERisk_A - \ERisk_B) = \big(\Var L_A + \Var L_B - 2\Cov(L_A, L_B)\big)/n'$. Explain why pairing helps when the

\item For the shrunk mean $a\,\bar y$, derive the optimal $a^*$ and show that the minimal mean squared error is

\end{derivations}
