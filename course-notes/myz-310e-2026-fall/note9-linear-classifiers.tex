\NoteTitle{9}{4}{Linear classifiers:\\ from the perceptron to the support vector machine}{Linear classifiers}{%
A linear classifier separates classes with a hyperplane. Many algorithms learn one --- nearest centroid, Gaussian
discriminant analysis, least squares, the perceptron, logistic regression, support vector machines --- and they differ
only in which hyperplane they choose. This note develops the geometry they share, then derives each algorithm step by
step, traces it by hand on a few points, gives its pseudocode and its running time (for Gaussian discriminant analysis
by reference to Note~6), and ends with what no hyperplane
can do. The perceptron receives special attention: its update rule, its convergence proof and its dual form lead
directly to the kernel methods of Note~10 and to the neurons of week~5.}

\section{What makes a classifier linear}\label{sec:def}

A binary classifier is \emph{linear} if its decision function has the form
\[
  f(\x) = \sign\big(g(\x)\big), \qquad g(\x) = \w\T\x + b ,
\]
with labels $t \in \{-1, +1\}$ (the $t = 2y - 1$ of Note~4, Section~2; in this note and in Note~10 the letter $t$ is the class label, while $y$ is kept for model outputs and regression targets), weight vector $\w \in \R^d$ and bias $b \in \R$; $g$ is the \emph{linear discriminant
function} or \emph{score}. For $m$ classes, $f(\x) = \argmax_c\, g_c(\x)$ with $g_c(\x) = \w_c\T\x + b_c$, that is
$f(\x) = \argmax_c (W\x + \vect{b})_c$ with the $\w_c\T$ as rows of $W \in \R^{m \times d}$. For $m = 2$ the two forms
agree: class~2 wins exactly when $\w_2\T\x + b_2 > \w_1\T\x + b_1$, i.e.\ when $(\w_2 - \w_1)\T\x + (b_2 - b_1) > 0$, a
binary rule with $\w = \w_2 - \w_1$ and $b = b_2 - b_1$. Only differences of scores matter.

\paragraph{Geometry} The decision boundary $H = \{\x : \w\T\x + b = 0\}$ is a hyperplane --- a line in two dimensions, a
plane in three. It splits $\R^d$ into the two half-spaces $\mathcal R_+ = \{\x : g(\x) > 0\}$ and $\mathcal R_- = \{\x :
g(\x) < 0\}$, the decision regions of the two classes. Two facts are used throughout the note.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{$\w$ is normal to $H$.} If $\x_A$ and $\x_B$ both lie on $H$, subtracting $\w\T\x_A + b = 0$ from
$\w\T\x_B + b = 0$ gives $\w\T(\x_B - \x_A) = 0$: $\w$ is orthogonal to every direction lying inside $H$.
\item \emph{$g$ measures distance.} Write any point as its orthogonal projection $\x_\perp$ onto $H$ plus a step $r$
along the unit normal, $\x = \x_\perp + r\,\w/\norm{\w}$. Applying $g$ and using $g(\x_\perp) = 0$,
$g(\x) = \w\T\x_\perp + b + r\,\w\T\w/\norm{\w} = r\norm{\w}$. Hence the signed distance of $\x$ to $H$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dist}
  r(\x) = \frac{\w\T\x + b}{\norm{\w}} ,
\end{equation}
positive on the side $\w$ points to. The origin is at signed distance $b/\norm{\w}$.
\end{enumerate}
Multiplying $(\w, b)$ by any $c > 0$ changes neither $H$ nor the classifier, only the scale of $g$; this freedom is
used to define the SVM in Section~\ref{sec:svm}. In the multiclass case each region
$\{\x : g_c(\x) > g_{c'}(\x)\ \forall c' \ne c\}$ is an intersection of half-spaces, hence convex: each condition
$g_c(\x) > g_{c'}(\x)$ reads $(\w_c - \w_{c'})\T\x + (b_c - b_{c'}) > 0$, which is itself a half-space.

\begin{worked}{distance to a line}
For $\w = (1, 2)$, $b = -4$ (the line $x_1 + 2x_2 = 4$) and $\x = (3, 3)$: $g(\x) = 5 > 0$, class $+1$, at distance
$5/\sqrt5 \approx 2.24$; the origin is at $-4/\sqrt5 \approx -1.79$. Doubling $(\w, b)$ doubles $g(\x)$ but not the
distances.
\end{worked}

\paragraph{The bias trick} As in Note~8, Section~1, we often absorb the bias: with $\xt = (1, x_1, \dots, x_d)\T$ and
$\wt = (b, w_1, \dots, w_d)\T$ we have $g(\x) = \wt\T\xt$. A hyperplane through the origin in $\R^{d+1}$ thus represents
any hyperplane in $\R^d$, and algorithms can be written without a separate bias.

\paragraph{Linear separability} A dataset is \emph{linearly separable} if some hyperplane classifies all of it
correctly: there exist $\w, b$ with $t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) > 0$ for every $i$. The quantity $t_i\,g(\x_i)$, the
\emph{margin} of example $i$, is positive exactly when the example is correctly classified, and a large negative value
means a confidently wrong prediction; it appears in every loss of this note. Many datasets are not separable; the
simplest one, XOR, is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:features}.

\paragraph{Label conventions} Binary labels are $t \in \{-1, +1\}$ everywhere except in Section~\ref{sec:logistic}, where
the probabilistic model is more natural with $t \in \{0, 1\}$. With $m$ classes the label is $t \in \{1, \dots, m\}$. The
two class means are written $\vmu_+$ and $\vmu_-$ (the $\vmu_1$ and $\vmu_0$ of Note~6).

\subsection{Nearest centroid}

Store the class means $\vmu_c = \frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i : t_i = c} \x_i$, where $n_c$ is the number of training examples of
class~$c$, and assign $\x$ to the nearest. Expanding the
squared distance,
\[
  \norm{\x - \vmu_c}^2 = \norm{\x}^2 - 2\vmu_c\T\x + \norm{\vmu_c}^2 ,
\]
and $\norm{\x}^2$ is the same for all classes, so minimising the distance is maximising $g_c(\x) = 2\vmu_c\T\x -
\norm{\vmu_c}^2$: the rule is linear with $\w_c = 2\vmu_c$ and $b_c = -\norm{\vmu_c}^2$. For two classes, halving the
difference of the two scores,
\[
  g(\x) = \tfrac12\big(\norm{\x - \vmu_-}^2 - \norm{\x - \vmu_+}^2\big) = (\vmu_+ - \vmu_-)\T\x
  - \tfrac12\big(\norm{\vmu_+}^2 - \norm{\vmu_-}^2\big) ;
\]
the boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the means. The method ignores the shape and spread
of the classes, which is why it misplaces the line in Figure~\ref{fig:linclass}.

\begin{worked}{nearest centroid by hand}
Class $A$ has points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 1)$ and class $B$ has $(5, 5)$, $(6, 4)$, $(7, 6)$. The centroids are
$\vmu_A = (2, 2)$ and $\vmu_B = (6, 5)$, with $\norm{\vmu_A}^2 = 8$ and $\norm{\vmu_B}^2 = 61$. With $B$ as the positive
class, $\w = \vmu_B - \vmu_A = (4, 3)$ and $b = -\frac12(61 - 8) = -26.5$, so $g(\x) = 4x_1 + 3x_2 - 26.5$. For the query
$\x = (4, 3)$, $g = 16 + 9 - 26.5 = -1.5 < 0$: class $A$. Check: $\norm{\x - \vmu_A}^2 = 5$ and $\norm{\x - \vmu_B}^2 = 8$,
and indeed $\frac12(5 - 8) = -1.5$. All six training points are classified correctly.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Gaussian discriminant analysis and Fisher's direction}

With Gaussian classes sharing one covariance $\Sigma$, the Bayes classifier is linear, with $\w = \Sigma^{-1}(\vmu_+ -
\vmu_-)$: this is \emph{linear discriminant analysis} (LDA, Note~6, Section~3, which also works out a two-dimensional
example by hand). With equal priors it is nearest centroid in the Mahalanobis metric, and it reduces to plain nearest
centroid when moreover $\Sigma \propto I$; unequal priors $P_+ \ne P_-$ shift the threshold $b$ by $\log(P_+/P_-)$ but
leave $\w$ unchanged. If each class has its own covariance, the boundary becomes quadratic (QDA, Note~6, Section~3) and
the classifier is no longer linear, so it falls outside this note.

Fisher~\cite{fisher1936} reached the same direction without any probability model. Project the data on $\w$ and compare
the squared distance between the projected class means, $(\w\T\Delta)^2$ with $\Delta = \vmu_+ - \vmu_-$, with the
projected spread within the classes, $\w\T S_W\w$, where
\[
  S_W = \sum_{c \in \{+, -\}}\ \sum_{i : t_i = c} (\x_i - \vmu_c)(\x_i - \vmu_c)\T
\]
is the \emph{pooled within-class scatter matrix}; $S_W/n$ is the pooled covariance estimate $\hat\Sigma$ of
Note~6 (dividing by $n - 2$ instead gives the unbiased version). Fisher maximises the ratio $F(\w) = (\w\T\Delta)^2/(\w\T S_W\w)$. Using $\nabla(\w\T\Delta)^2 =
2(\w\T\Delta)\,\Delta$ and $\nabla(\w\T S_W\w) = 2S_W\w$ (as $S_W$ is symmetric), the quotient rule gives
\[
  \nabla F = \frac{2(\w\T\Delta)\,\Delta\,(\w\T S_W\w) - (\w\T\Delta)^2\, 2S_W\w}{(\w\T S_W\w)^2} ,
\]
and $\nabla F = 0$ requires the numerator to vanish, i.e.\ $S_W\w = c\,\Delta$ with the scalar $c = (\w\T S_W\w)/(\w\T\Delta)$.
Hence $\w \propto S_W^{-1}\Delta \propto \hat\Sigma^{-1}\Delta$, the LDA direction (the length of $\w$ does not matter,
since $F$ is unchanged by rescaling $\w$); the threshold $b$ is chosen afterwards, for example midway between the projected
means. Computing it is the LDA fit of Note~6, Section~3 (``Fitting and cost''), where the pseudocode and the running time are given:
$O(nd^2)$ to form $S_W$ and $O(d^3)$ to solve $S_W\w = \Delta$.

\subsection{Least squares on \texorpdfstring{$\pm 1$}{plus-minus one} targets}

The labels $t_i = \pm1$ are numbers, so linear regression (Note~8, Section~2) can be fitted to them directly, and the
sign of the fitted value used as the predicted class: $\wt = (\Xt\T\Xt)^{-1}\Xt\T\vect{t}$, where $\Xt$ is the $n \times (d +
1)$ matrix with rows $\xt_i\T$ (Note~8, Section~1) and the targets $t_i = \pm1$ --- called $y_i$ in Note~8 --- are stacked in
$\vect{t}$. Ridge regression (Note~8, Section~4) can be used as well; it changes the closed form to $(\Xt\T\Xt +
\lambda I)^{-1}\Xt\T\vect{t}$ but not the defect discussed next. For $m$ classes, regress the one-hot vectors and take
the largest output. The method is fast and has a closed form, but its loss is wrong for classification. Written in terms
of the margin $z_i = t_i\,g(\x_i)$, and using $g(\x_i) - t_i = t_i\big(t_i\,g(\x_i) - 1\big)$, which holds because $t_i^2 = 1$,
\[
  (g(\x_i) - t_i)^2 = t_i^2\,(t_i\,g(\x_i) - 1)^2 = (1 - z_i)^2 ,
\]
which grows again for $z_i > 1$: a point that is correctly classified \emph{with a large margin} is penalised as if it
were an error (Figure~\ref{fig:losses}). A distant cluster of easy examples therefore pulls the boundary towards
itself. In Figure~\ref{fig:linclass} the twelve far-away amber points tilt the least-squares line and triple its training
error (9.8\%) compared with logistic regression (3.3\%); fitted without those twelve points, least squares also makes
3.3\% errors on all 122.

\begin{worked}{one easy point moves the boundary}
In one dimension, take $x = -2, -1$ with $t = -1$ and $x = 1, 2$ with $t = +1$. By symmetry the fit $y = wx + b$ has $b = 0$,
$w = \sum x_i t_i / \sum x_i^2 = 6/10$, and the boundary is at $x = 0$, midway between the classes. Now add one positive
point at $x = 10$, as correct as a point can be. The normal equations (Note~8) with $n = 5$, $\sum x_i = 10$, $\sum x_i^2 =
110$, $\sum t_i = 1$, $\sum x_i t_i = 16$ read
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 10 & 110 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} b \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 16 \end{pmatrix}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad b = -\tfrac19, \quad w = \tfrac{7}{45}, \quad \text{boundary } x = -b/w = \tfrac57 \approx 0.71 .
\]
The boundary moved by $0.71$ towards the positive class, because the fit tries to bring the prediction at $x = 10$
(here $13/9 \approx 1.44$) down to $1$ by flattening the line. With ten copies of the point at $x = 10$ the boundary
reaches $x \approx 1.03$ and the positive point at $x = 1$ becomes an error. Logistic regression and the SVM, whose losses
vanish (or almost) for large margins, are hardly affected.
\end{worked}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Nearest centroid and the least-squares classifier.}\label{alg:centroid}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{TrainCentroid}{$\{(\x_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, classes $1, \dots, m$}
  \For{$c = 1, \dots, m$} $\vmu_c \gets$ mean of the $\x_i$ with $t_i = c$; \ $\w_c \gets 2\vmu_c$; \ $b_c \gets -\norm{\vmu_c}^2$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\{(\w_c, b_c)\}$ \Comment{predict $\argmax_c (\w_c\T\x + b_c)$}
\EndFunction
\Function{TrainLeastSquares}{$\Xt \in \R^{n \times (d+1)}$, targets $T$ ($\pm1$ vector, or one-hot $n \times m$)}
  \State \Return $\tilde W \gets$ \Call{LstSq}{$\Xt$, $T$} \Comment{QR or SVD (Note~8); $\tilde W$: $(d + 1) \times m$, or $\wt$}
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\textit{predict} $\sign(\wt\T\xt)$, or $\argmax_c (\tilde W\T\xt)_c$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{nearest centroid and least-squares classifier}
\emph{Nearest centroid}: training $O(nd)$, one pass to sum the points of each class; prediction $O(md)$ per query, one
inner product per class; memory $O(md)$ for the centroids. It is the cheapest classifier in this note.
\emph{Least squares}: training $O(nd^2)$ for the QR factorisation of the $n \times (d+1)$ matrix, plus $O(ndm)$ for $m$
one-hot targets; through the normal equations it is $O(nd^2)$ to form $\Xt\T\Xt$ and $O(d^3)$ to solve, which only matters
for $d$ in the thousands. Prediction $O(md)$; memory $O(nd)$ during training, $O(md)$ for the model.
\end{runtime}

\subsection{Why not minimise the error rate directly}\label{sec:surrogates}

The training error rate $\frac1n\sum_i \ind{t_i\,g(\x_i) \le 0}$ is piecewise constant in $(\w, b)$: a small change of
the parameters changes no prediction, so its gradient is zero almost everywhere and gradient descent (Note~7) cannot
move; minimising it exactly is a hard combinatorial problem. The remaining algorithms minimise the \emph{surrogate}
losses of the margin $z = t\,g(\x)$ introduced in Note~4, Section~2 --- the squared loss $(1 - z)^2$ (least squares,
above), the hinge $\max(0, 1 - z)$ (the SVM, Section~\ref{sec:svm}) and the logistic loss $\log(1 + e^{-z})$ (logistic
regression, Section~\ref{sec:logistic}) --- plus one more that is new here: the \emph{perceptron loss} $\max(0, -z)$
(Section~\ref{sec:perceptron}), zero for every correct point and growing linearly with the depth of a mistake. Figure~\ref{fig:losses} adds it to the curves of Note~4: unlike the hinge it asks for no safety margin,
and unlike the squared loss it never penalises a correct point.

\section{The perceptron}\label{sec:perceptron}

Rosenblatt's perceptron~\cite{rosenblatt1958}, first simulated on a computer in 1957--58 and then built in hardware as
the Mark~I Perceptron (publicly demonstrated in 1960), was one of the first artificial neurons trained from data.

\subsection{The algorithm and its logic}

The rule is: look at one example at a time; if it is classified correctly, do nothing; if not, add it to the weights (or
subtract it, for a negative example). Algorithm~\ref{alg:perceptron} states it with the bias written separately. A score
of exactly zero counts as a mistake, which is what gets the algorithm started from $\w = \vect{0}$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The perceptron (primal form), with the averaged and pocket variants of Section~\ref{sec:avgperc}.}\label{alg:perceptron}
\label{alg:avgperceptron}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require examples $(\x_i, t_i)$, $t_i \in \{-1, +1\}$; maximum number of epochs $E$
\Ensure weights $\w$ and bias $b$ (or their averages $\bar{\w}$, $\bar b$, or the pocket weights)
\State $\w \gets \vect{0}$, \ $b \gets 0$; \quad $\vect{s} \gets \vect{0}$, \ $s_b \gets 0$, \ $N \gets 0$
\Comment{sums for the averaged variant}
\State $\mathit{pocket} \gets (\w, b)$, \ $\mathit{best} \gets n$ \Comment{pocket variant only}
\For{epoch $= 1, \dots, E$}
  \State $\mathit{mistakes} \gets 0$
  \For{each $i$, in random order} \Comment{cyclic order in the trace below}
    \If{$t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) \le 0$} \Comment{wrong side, or on the boundary}
      \State $\w \gets \w + t_i\x_i$, \quad $b \gets b + t_i$, \quad $\mathit{mistakes} \gets \mathit{mistakes} + 1$
    \EndIf
    \State $\vect{s} \gets \vect{s} + \w$, \ $s_b \gets s_b + b$, \ $N \gets N + 1$ \Comment{averaged variant only}
  \EndFor
  \State $e \gets$ number of $i$ with $t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) \le 0$ \Comment{pocket only; one extra pass, $O(nd)$}
  \If{$e < \mathit{best}$} $\mathit{pocket} \gets (\w, b)$, \ $\mathit{best} \gets e$ \EndIf
  \If{$\mathit{mistakes} = 0$} \textbf{break} \Comment{all training points correct}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $(\w, b)$; or $(\bar{\w}, \bar b) = (\vect{s}/N,\, s_b/N)$ (averaged); or $\mathit{pocket}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{What one update does} In the augmented notation the update is $\wt \gets \wt + t_i\xt_i$. The new margin of
the example that triggered it is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:percgain}
  t_i\,(\wt + t_i\xt_i)\T\xt_i = t_i\,\wt\T\xt_i + t_i^2\norm{\xt_i}^2 = t_i\,\wt\T\xt_i + \norm{\x_i}^2 + 1 ,
\end{equation}
so every update raises the margin of the offending example by exactly $\norm{\x_i}^2 + 1 > 0$ (the $+1$ comes from the
constant feature, i.e.\ from the bias). It moves the example towards the correct side, though not necessarily all the
way across: one update is a correction, not a guarantee. Geometrically, for a positive example $\w$ is rotated
\emph{towards} $\x_i$ and for a negative one away from it; the boundary, perpendicular to $\w$, turns accordingly
(Figure~\ref{fig:perceptron}a). The price is that other examples' margins change too, by $t_j t_i\,\xt_j\T\xt_i$, which
may be negative: an update can spoil a point that was correct.

\subsection{The perceptron criterion}

The update is stochastic gradient descent (Note~7, Section~5) on the \emph{perceptron criterion}
\[
  J_{\mathrm p}(\w, b) = \sum_{i=1}^n \max\big(0,\, -t_i(\w\T\x_i + b)\big) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal M} -t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) ,
\]
where $\mathcal M$ is the set of misclassified examples; each term is proportional to the distance of a misclassified
point to the boundary \eqref{eq:dist}. For $i \in \mathcal M$ the term is linear in the parameters, with derivatives
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \w}\big[-t_i(\w\T\x_i + b)\big] = -t_i\x_i , \qquad
  \frac{\partial}{\partial b}\big[-t_i(\w\T\x_i + b)\big] = -t_i ,
\]
and zero for a correct example. A stochastic gradient step on the term of one example is therefore $\w \gets \w +
\eta\,t_i\x_i$, $b \gets b + \eta\,t_i$ if the example is wrong, and nothing otherwise. The step size does not matter: starting from zero, every iterate with step $\eta$ is $\eta$ times the
iterate with step $1$, and the signs of all scores --- hence all decisions --- are the same. This is why the classical
algorithm simply uses $\eta = 1$. Descending the full gradient of $J_{\mathrm p}$ instead gives the \emph{batch
perceptron}, $\w \gets \w + \eta\sum_{i \in \mathcal M} t_i\x_i$, $b \gets b + \eta\sum_{i \in \mathcal M} t_i$, one step per
pass over the data at $O(nd)$; restricting the sum to the misclassified points of a mini-batch gives the mini-batch version.
Note that $J_{\mathrm p}$ is also zero at the useless point $\w = \vect{0}$, $b = 0$;
treating a zero score as a mistake is what keeps the algorithm away from it.

\paragraph{Reading the trace} Each mistake raises the margin of its own example by $\norm{\x_i}^2 + 1$, as in
\eqref{eq:percgain}: at step~1 from $0$ to $6$, at step~4 from $-2$ to $3$, at step~7 from $-2$ to $9$, at step~8 from
$-4$ to $1$. At step~2 the margin of $\x_2$ rises from $-1$ to $0$ only, since $\norm{\xt_2}^2 = 1$: the point is still not
correct, and is fixed only later, through the updates on other points. Step~4 corrects $\x_4$ but spoils $\x_3$
(its score falls from $5$ to $-2$), which costs step~7, and step~7 in turn spoils $\x_4$. After five mistakes the third
epoch is clean and the algorithm stops with $g(\x) = 3x_2 - 1$: the horizontal line $x_2 = 1/3$. It separates the data, but
passes at distance $1/3$ from $\x_2$ and $\x_4$ while the positive points are far away --- the perceptron is satisfied with
\emph{any} separating line. Another visiting order gives another line.

\subsection{Convergence on separable data}

\paragraph{Theorem (Novikoff~\cite{novikoff1962})} Suppose $\norm{\xt_i} \le R$ for all $i$ and the data are separable
with margin $\gamma > 0$ in the augmented space: some unit vector $\wt^*$ has $t_i\,\wt^{*\mathsf T}\xt_i \ge \gamma$ for
all $i$. Then the perceptron, started at zero and visiting the examples in any order, makes at most $(R/\gamma)^2$
mistakes.

\paragraph{Proof sketch} Let $\wt_k$ be the weights after $k$ mistakes, $\wt_0 = \vect{0}$, and suppose mistake $k+1$
occurs on example $i$. Two inequalities follow from the update $\wt_{k+1} = \wt_k + t_i\xt_i$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The alignment with $\wt^*$ grows linearly: $\wt^{*\mathsf T}\wt_{k+1} = \wt^{*\mathsf T}\wt_k +
t_i\,\wt^{*\mathsf T}\xt_i \ge \wt^{*\mathsf T}\wt_k + \gamma$, hence $\wt^{*\mathsf T}\wt_k \ge k\gamma$.
\item The length grows at most like $\sqrt{k}$: $\norm{\wt_{k+1}}^2 = \norm{\wt_k}^2 + 2t_i\,\wt_k\T\xt_i +
\norm{\xt_i}^2 \le \norm{\wt_k}^2 + R^2$, because $t_i\,\wt_k\T\xt_i \le 0$ on a mistake. Hence $\norm{\wt_k}^2 \le kR^2$.
\end{enumerate}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $k\gamma \le \wt^{*\mathsf T}\wt_k \le \norm{\wt^*}\,\norm{\wt_k} \le \sqrt{k}\,R$,
so $\sqrt{k} \le R/\gamma$ and $k \le (R/\gamma)^2$. The intuition: the weights keep turning towards $\wt^*$ by a fixed
amount per mistake, while their length grows more slowly; the cosine between $\wt_k$ and $\wt^*$ would exceed $1$ after
more than $(R/\gamma)^2$ mistakes. \qed

The bound depends on neither $n$ nor $d$, only on the geometry of the data. In the trace above, $R^2 = \max_i
\norm{\xt_i}^2 = 11$, and $\wt^* = (-1, 0, 2)/\sqrt5$ (that is $b = -1$, $\w = (0, 2)$, rescaled) gives every point a
margin of at least $\gamma = 1/\sqrt5 \approx 0.45$; the bound is $11 \times 5 = 55$ mistakes, and five were needed. Note
that $\gamma$ is measured in the augmented space, where $(b, \w)$ is normalised as one vector, so it is not the Euclidean
distance seen in the plot: the line $x_2 = 1/2$ of this $\wt^*$ is at distance $1/2$ from the closest points. The bound is
loose, but it says something important: well-separated data (large $\gamma$) are learned with few mistakes.

\subsection{Non-separable data: pocket and averaged perceptron}\label{sec:avgperc}

If the data are not separable, some example is always misclassified and the perceptron never stops; its weights keep
jumping, and the final iterate can be poor even when some earlier one was good. On the data of Figure~\ref{fig:linclass} it
made 606 corrections in 100 passes. Two classical remedies return a better, more stable answer. Gallant's \emph{pocket
algorithm}~\cite{gallant1990} keeps ``in its pocket'' the weights that survived the longest run of consecutive correct
classifications; in its \emph{ratchet} version the pocket is replaced only if the training error is also lower.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:perceptron} uses the simplest form of the idea: once per epoch it counts the training errors and keeps
the best weights seen so far, at the cost of one extra pass over the data. The \emph{averaged perceptron} returns the
average of the weight vectors over all steps (the extra lines of Algorithm~\ref{alg:perceptron}): weights that survived
many steps without a mistake count more, and the average fluctuates far less than the last iterate. It is the practical
approximation of the \emph{voted perceptron} of Freund and Schapire~\cite{freund1999}, which in their experiments came
close to an SVM at the cost of a perceptron.

\subsection{The dual form}\label{sec:dualperc}

Since the weights start at zero and only ever add $t_i\xt_i$, after training
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dualw}
  \wt = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i t_i\,\xt_i , \qquad \alpha_i = \text{number of mistakes made on example } i .
\end{equation}
In the trace, $\alpha = (1, 1, 1, 2)$ and indeed $1\cdot(1, 1, 2) - 1\cdot(1, 0, 0) + 1\cdot(1, 3, 1) - 2\cdot(1, 2, 0) =
(-1, 0, 3)$, i.e.\ $b = -1$, $\w = (0, 3)$. The score of any point is then
\[
  g(\x) = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j t_j\, \xt_j\T\xt ,
\]
so the algorithm can be run entirely on the counts $\alpha_j$ and on inner products between examples, without ever
forming $\wt$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dualperc}). Examples that never caused a mistake have $\alpha_j = 0$ and drop out. Nothing
is gained in this form for plain vectors, but it is the gateway to non-linear classification: replace every inner product
$\xt_j\T\xt$ by a \emph{kernel} $k(\x_j, \x)$ and the same few lines become the kernel perceptron of Note~10. The SVM has
the same structure (Section~\ref{sec:svmdual}).

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The perceptron in dual form.}\label{alg:dualperc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require examples $(\x_i, t_i)$, epochs $E$
\State $K_{ij} \gets \xt_i\T\xt_j$ for all $i, j$ \Comment{Gram matrix, $n \times n$}
\State $\alpha \gets \vect{0} \in \R^n$
\For{epoch $= 1, \dots, E$}
  \State $\mathit{mistakes} \gets 0$
  \For{each $i$ in random order}
    \If{$t_i \sum_j \alpha_j t_j K_{ji} \le 0$} \Comment{the same test as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:perceptron}}
      \State $\alpha_i \gets \alpha_i + 1$, \quad $\mathit{mistakes} \gets \mathit{mistakes} + 1$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{$\mathit{mistakes} = 0$} \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\alpha$ \Comment{predict with $\sign\big(\sum_j \alpha_j t_j\,\xt_j\T\xt\big)$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{perceptron (primal, averaged, dual)}
\emph{Primal}: each visit costs one inner product, $O(d)$, and an update another $O(d)$; one epoch is $O(nd)$ and $E$
epochs $O(End)$. On separable data at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ updates happen in total, whatever $n$. Prediction $O(d)$;
memory $O(d)$ beyond the data, which can be streamed from disk. \emph{Averaged}: the same order, $O(nd)$ per epoch, with one
extra vector of memory (the sum can even be maintained lazily, touching only the non-zero features of sparse inputs).
\emph{Pocket}: one error count per epoch costs another $O(nd)$, so the order stays $O(nd)$ per epoch; checking after every
update instead would cost up to $O(n^2d)$ per epoch. Memory: one extra vector. \emph{Dual}: $O(n^2 d)$ to compute the Gram matrix and $O(n^2)$ memory to store it, then $O(n)$ per visit and $O(n^2)$ per
epoch; prediction $O(n_\alpha d)$, with $n_\alpha$ the number of examples with $\alpha_j > 0$. The dual is worth its
quadratic cost only when kernels are used (Note~10).
\end{runtime}

\section{Logistic regression}\label{sec:logistic}

Logistic regression~\cite{cox1958} fits a model of a probability, but it is used as a classifier. It is the discriminative counterpart of the Gaussian
classifiers of Note~6: instead of modelling the classes and applying Bayes' rule, it models the class probability
directly. In this section the labels are $t \in \{0, 1\}$.

\subsection{The model}

For two classes Bayes' rule can always be written as $P(t = 1 \mid \x) = p(\x \mid 1)P_1 / [p(\x \mid 1)P_1 +
p(\x \mid 0)P_0] = 1/(1 + e^{-a(\x)})$, with the priors $P_c = P(t = c)$ and the log-odds $a(\x) = \log[p(\x \mid
1)P_1/p(\x \mid 0)P_0]$. Under the LDA
assumptions $a(\x)$ is exactly linear (Note~6, Section~3). Logistic regression keeps this form and drops the assumptions:
it postulates
\[
  P(t = 1 \mid \x) = \sigma(\w\T\x + b) = \sigma(\wt\T\xt), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} ,
\]
so that the log-odds are linear in $\x$: solving $P = \sigma(a)$ for $a$ gives $a = \operatorname{logit}(P) = \log[P/(1 -
P)]$. The sigmoid is thus the inverse of the \emph{logit link}, which makes logistic regression a \emph{generalised linear
model}: a linear predictor passed through a fixed link to the mean of the label. The logistic sigmoid maps $\R$ to $(0, 1)$, is a smooth version of a step function, and has two properties used below:
\[
  1 - \sigma(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(-z), \qquad
  \sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\,\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) .
\]
Since $\sigma(z) > \frac12$ exactly when $z > 0$, predicting the more probable class is still $\sign(\w\T\x + b)$: the
boundary is a hyperplane. What logistic regression adds is a probability, and a loss that does not punish confident
correct predictions. In neural terms it is a single neuron with a sigmoid activation: the sigmoid is a differentiable
stand-in for the perceptron's step, and a rough model of how a biological neuron's firing rate saturates as its input
grows (week~5 builds networks of such units).

\subsection{Maximum likelihood and the gradient, step by step}

\emph{A warning on notation.} In Note~8 the letter $y_i$ was the regression target; in this note (as in Bishop's
book~\cite{bishop2006}) targets are always $t_i$, and $y_i$ is the model's output. Write $y_i = \sigma(a_i)$ with $a_i =
\wt\T\xt_i$ for the predicted probability (the $g$ of Note~4, Section~2). The probability of label $t_i$ is $y_i^{t_i}(1 - y_i)^{1 - t_i}$, and the
negative log-likelihood of independent labels (Note~2, Section~2) is the cross-entropy (Note~4, Section~2)
\[
  J(\wt) = \sum_{i=1}^n J_i, \qquad J_i = -\big[t_i \log y_i + (1 - t_i)\log(1 - y_i)\big] .
\]
In terms of the $\pm1$ margin $z_i = (2t_i - 1)a_i$ this is $J_i = \log(1 + e^{-z_i})$, the logistic curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:losses}. Indeed, for $t_i = 1$, $J_i = -\log\sigma(a_i) = \log(1 + e^{-a_i})$, and for $t_i = 0$, $J_i =
-\log(1 - \sigma(a_i)) = -\log\sigma(-a_i) = \log(1 + e^{a_i})$; both read $\log(1 + e^{-z_i})$, with $z_i = a_i$ and
$z_i = -a_i$ respectively. The gradient follows from the chain rule $J_i \to y_i \to a_i \to \wt$:
\[
  \frac{\partial J_i}{\partial y_i} = -\frac{t_i}{y_i} + \frac{1 - t_i}{1 - y_i} = \frac{y_i - t_i}{y_i(1 - y_i)} , \qquad
  \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial a_i} = y_i(1 - y_i) , \qquad
  \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial \wt} = \xt_i .
\]
The factor $y_i(1 - y_i)$ cancels, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loggrad}
  \nabla J = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - t_i)\,\xt_i = \Xt\T(\vect{y} - \vect{t}) .
\end{equation}
This has the same form as the least-squares gradient of Note~8 (with Note~8's target $y_i$ renamed $t_i$),
\emph{prediction minus target, times input}, but now the prediction is a probability. Each example pushes $\wt$ in proportion to how wrong its probability is: a confidently correct point
($y_i \approx t_i$) contributes almost nothing, which is why distant easy points do not pull the boundary.

\begin{worked}{one gradient step}
Six points on a line, $x = -2, -1, -1, 1, 1, 2$ with labels $t = 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1$; the classes overlap, since one
positive sits at $x = -1$ and one negative at $x = 1$. Start from $\wt = (b, w) = (0, 0)$: every $y_i = \sigma(0) = \frac12$
and $J = 6\log 2 \approx 4.159$. The residuals $y_i - t_i$ are $\frac12, \frac12, -\frac12, \frac12, -\frac12, -\frac12$, so
by \eqref{eq:loggrad}
\[
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \textstyle\sum_i (y_i - t_i) = 0, \qquad
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \textstyle\sum_i (y_i - t_i)x_i = -1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 - 1 = -2 .
\]
A gradient step with $\eta = 0.25$ gives $w = 0 - 0.25 \times (-2) = 0.5$, $b = 0$, and the loss drops to $3.523$. The
bias stays zero at every step here because the data are symmetric under $x \to -x$, $t \to 1 - t$; the boundary is at
$x = 0$ and only the steepness of the sigmoid is being learned.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Training: gradient descent and Newton's method}

The Hessian follows by differentiating \eqref{eq:loggrad} once more. In the term $(y_i - t_i)\,\xt_i$ only $y_i$ depends on
$\wt$, with $\partial y_i/\partial\wt = y_i(1 - y_i)\,\xt_i$, so its derivative is the outer product $\xt_i\,(\partial
y_i/\partial\wt)\T = y_i(1 - y_i)\,\xt_i\xt_i\T$, and
\[
  \Hess = \nabla^2 J = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i(1 - y_i)\,\xt_i\xt_i\T = \Xt\T S\,\Xt, \qquad S = \operatorname{diag}\big(y_i(1 - y_i)\big) .
\]
(We write the Hessian as $\Hess$ to keep $H$ for the decision boundary.) For any vector $\vect{v}$, $\vect{v}\T \Hess\vect{v}
= \sum_i y_i(1 - y_i)(\vect{v}\T\xt_i)^2 \ge 0$: $J$ is convex (Note~7, Section~4), so every local minimum is global. But
$\nabla J = \vect{0}$ is non-linear in $\wt$ and has no closed-form solution, so the minimum is found iteratively. Gradient
descent (Algorithm~\ref{alg:logreg}) uses $\wt \gets \wt - \eta\,\Xt\T(\vect{y} - \vect{t})$, or the same with mini-batches
for large $n$ (Note~7, Section~5). Newton's method (Note~7, Section~6) uses the curvature: $\wt \gets \wt - \Hess^{-1}\nabla
J$. Writing $\wt = \Hess^{-1}\Hess\wt$, inserting $S S^{-1} = I$ in the gradient and factoring out $\Xt\T S$,
\[
  \wt - \Hess^{-1}\nabla J = \Hess^{-1}\big(\Xt\T S\Xt\,\wt - \Xt\T S\,S^{-1}(\vect{y} - \vect{t})\big)
  = \Hess^{-1}\Xt\T S\big(\Xt\wt - S^{-1}(\vect{y} - \vect{t})\big) = (\Xt\T S\Xt)^{-1}\Xt\T S\,\vect{u} ,
\]
with the \emph{working targets} $\vect{u} = \Xt\wt - S^{-1}(\vect{y} - \vect{t})$. Compare with \emph{weighted} least
squares: minimising $\sum_i s_i(\wt\T\xt_i - u_i)^2 = (\Xt\wt - \vect{u})\T S(\Xt\wt - \vect{u})$ and setting the gradient
to zero gives $\Xt\T S\Xt\,\wt = \Xt\T S\,\vect{u}$, the normal equations of Note~8 with row $i$ weighted by $s_i$. So each
Newton step is a weighted least-squares fit to the working targets $\vect{u}$ with weights $s_i = y_i(1 - y_i)$, hence the
name \emph{iteratively reweighted least squares} (IRLS). Points whose probability is already close to 0 or 1 receive little
weight. Methods between the two extremes, such as conjugate gradient and quasi-Newton methods like L-BFGS, are named in
Note~7, Section~6, and work equally well here.

\begin{worked}{Newton against gradient descent}
For the six points above, at $\wt = \vect{0}$ all weights are $\frac14$, so $\Hess = \frac14\sum_i \xt_i\xt_i\T =
\frac14\begin{pmatrix} 6 & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$, and
the first Newton step is $\wt = -\Hess^{-1}\nabla J = -(0/1.5,\ -2/3) = (0,\ 0.667)$. Iterating,

Newton reaches the minimum $w^* = 0.756$ to three digits in three steps; gradient descent needs about ten
($w_9 = 0.7557$, $w_{10} = 0.7560$). The fitted model says
$P(t = 1 \mid x = 2) = \sigma(1.51) = 0.82$ and $P(t = 1 \mid x = 1) = 0.68$: the overlap of the classes near $x = \pm1$ is
reflected in moderate probabilities.
\end{worked}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Logistic regression (gradient descent and Newton/IRLS) and softmax regression.}\label{alg:logreg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\Xt \in \R^{n \times (d+1)}$, $\vect{t} \in \{0, 1\}^n$, ridge $\lambda \ge 0$ (see below), step $\eta$ (GD), tolerance $\epsilon$
\State $\wt \gets \vect{0}$; \ $D \gets \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \dots, 1)$ \Comment{the bias is not penalised}
\Repeat
  \State $\vect{y} \gets \sigma(\Xt\wt)$ \Comment{element-wise}
  \State $\vect{g} \gets \Xt\T(\vect{y} - \vect{t}) + \lambda D\wt$
  \If{gradient descent} $\wt \gets \wt - \eta\,\vect{g}$
  \Else \ $S \gets \operatorname{diag}(y_i(1 - y_i))$; \ $\Hess \gets \Xt\T S\Xt + \lambda D$; \ $\wt \gets \wt - \Hess^{-1}\vect{g}$
  \Comment{Newton: solve, do not invert}
  \EndIf
\Until{$\norm{\vect{g}} < \epsilon$}
\State \Return $\wt$ \Comment{predict $\sigma(\wt\T\xt)$, class $\ind{\wt\T\xt > 0}$}
\Statex
\Function{Softmax}{$\Xt$, one-hot $T \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times m}$, $\lambda$, $\eta$, $\epsilon$} \Comment{$m$ classes, Section~\ref{sec:softmax}}
  \State $\tilde W \gets \vect{0} \in \R^{(d+1) \times m}$ \Comment{column $c$ is $\wt_c$}
  \Repeat
    \State $A \gets \Xt\tilde W$; \ $Y_{ic} \gets e^{A_{ic}} / \sum_k e^{A_{ik}}$ \Comment{row-wise softmax; subtract $\max_k A_{ik}$ first}
    \State $G \gets \Xt\T(Y - T) + \lambda D\tilde W$; \ $\tilde W \gets \tilde W - \eta\,G$
  \Until{$\norm{G} < \epsilon$}
  \State \Return $\tilde W$ \Comment{predict $\argmax_c (\tilde W\T\xt)_c$}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Separable data and regularisation} If the classes are separable, every margin can be made positive, and
multiplying $\wt$ by any $c > 1$ then lowers every term $\log(1 + e^{-z_i})$. The loss has no minimiser: $\norm{\wt}$ grows
without bound and the sigmoid tends to a step, with probabilities of exactly 0 and 1 --- overconfidence. Adding a ridge
penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\w}^2$ (Note~8, Section~4), as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:logreg}, restores a unique finite
solution; $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter chosen by validation (Note~4).

\subsection{More than two classes: softmax regression}\label{sec:softmax}

With $m$ classes, give each class a score $a_c = \wt_c\T\xt$ and turn the scores into probabilities with the
\emph{softmax}
\[
  y_c = P(t = c \mid \x) = \frac{e^{a_c}}{\sum_{k=1}^m e^{a_k}} .
\]
With a one-hot target vector $\vect{t}$ the cross-entropy of one example is $J = -\sum_c t_c \log y_c = -a_{\mathrm{true}} +
\log\sum_k e^{a_k}$. Differentiating with respect to the score $a_k$,
\[
  \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_k} = -t_k + \frac{e^{a_k}}{\sum_{k'} e^{a_{k'}}} = y_k - t_k , \qquad
  \nabla_{\wt_k} J = (y_k - t_k)\,\xt ,
\]
the same ``prediction minus target'' form as before. A gradient step raises the score of the true class and lowers every
other score in proportion to the probability it wrongly received. For $m = 2$ the softmax reduces to the sigmoid of the
score difference, $y_2 = e^{a_2}/(e^{a_1} + e^{a_2}) = \sigma(a_2 - a_1)$, so softmax regression generalises logistic regression; since adding the same
vector to every $\wt_c$ leaves all probabilities unchanged, one class can be fixed at zero, or a ridge penalty used.

\begin{worked}{softmax and its gradient}
Scores $\vect{a} = (2, 1, 0)$ give $e^{\vect a} = (7.389, 2.718, 1)$, sum $11.107$, so $\vect{y} = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$.
If the true class is the second, the loss is $-\log 0.245 = 1.41$ and $\vect{y} - \vect{t} = (0.665, -0.755, 0.090)$: the
step mostly lowers class~1 and raises class~2; class~3, already improbable, is barely touched. Concretely, if these
scores come from the input $\xt = (1, 2)$ (bias feature and one coordinate), a step with $\eta = 0.2$ changes each column
by $\Delta\wt_k = -\eta\,(y_k - t_k)\,\xt$ and hence each score by $-\eta\,(y_k - t_k)\norm{\xt}^2 = -(y_k - t_k)$: the new
scores are $(1.335, 1.755, -0.090)$, and the true class now has the highest.
\end{worked}

\begin{runtime}{logistic and softmax regression}
\emph{Gradient descent}: each iteration computes $\Xt\wt$ and $\Xt\T(\vect{y} - \vect{t})$, two matrix--vector products,
$O(nd)$; $T$ iterations cost $O(Tnd)$, with $T$ from tens to thousands depending on the conditioning (Note~7). Mini-batch
SGD with mini-batch size $n'$ costs $O(n'd)$ per step and suits data that do not fit in memory. \emph{Newton/IRLS}: forming
$\Xt\T S\Xt$ costs $O(nd^2)$ and solving the system $O(d^3)$ per iteration; typically 5 to 10 iterations suffice. Newton is
the method of choice up to $d$ of a few thousand; beyond, gradient methods or L-BFGS (a quasi-Newton method that
approximates the inverse Hessian from the last few gradients, Note~7). \emph{Softmax}: $O(ndm)$ per gradient
iteration (the product $\Xt\tilde W$), and $O(nm)$ for the softmax itself. Prediction is $O(d)$ ($O(dm)$ for softmax), and
all of it vectorises well on GPUs. \emph{Memory} beyond the data: $O(d)$ for gradient descent ($O(dm)$ for softmax) plus
$O(n)$ for $\vect{y}$, or $O(n'd)$ for a mini-batch when the data are streamed; Newton needs $O(d^2)$ for the Hessian, which is
what rules it out for very large $d$.
\end{runtime}

\begin{modern}{every neural classifier ends in logistic regression}
The last layer of an image classifier or a language model is a linear map followed by a softmax: multiclass logistic
regression on features computed by the layers below, trained with exactly the gradient $\vect{y} - \vect{t}$ derived
above. Conversely, training only a logistic regression on the frozen features of a pre-trained network --- a \emph{linear
probe} --- is a standard way to measure what those features contain~\cite{alain2017}; CLIP, for instance, was evaluated
with linear probes on 27 datasets~\cite{radford2021}. Everything in this section therefore applies on top of the most
modern representations.
\end{modern}

\section{Support vector machines}\label{sec:svm}

The perceptron stops at any separating hyperplane; Table~\ref{tab:trace} ended with a line that nearly touches two
points. Among all separating hyperplanes, the \emph{maximum-margin} one stays as far as possible from the closest training
points, so that small perturbations of the data --- noise, a slightly different test point --- do not cross it. Support
vector machines compute this hyperplane. The maximum-margin hyperplane goes back to Vapnik and
Lerner's ``generalized portrait'' of 1963~\cite{vapnik1963}; the modern algorithm is that of Boser, Guyon and
Vapnik~\cite{boser1992}, in the soft-margin form of Cortes and Vapnik~\cite{cortes1995}. Labels are again $t \in \{-1, +1\}$.

\subsection{Margins and the hard-margin SVM}

The \emph{functional margin} of an example is $t_i\,g(\x_i)$: positive if it is correctly classified, large if
confidently so. Its \emph{geometric margin} $t_i\,g(\x_i)/\norm{\w}$ is its signed distance to the boundary
\eqref{eq:dist}; the margin of the classifier is the smallest of these. Rescaling $(\w, b)$ by $c > 0$ multiplies
functional margins by $c$ but leaves geometric margins unchanged. We may therefore fix the scale so that the closest points have functional margin exactly one,
$\min_i t_i\,g(\x_i) = 1$. These points lie on the two hyperplanes $g(\x) = \pm1$, which by \eqref{eq:dist} are at
distance $1/\norm{\w}$ on either side of $H$. To keep the words apart: the \emph{margin} of the classifier is
$1/\norm{\w}$, the distance from $H$ to the closest points, and the empty band between the two hyperplanes has
\emph{width} $2/\norm{\w}$.

\paragraph{The hard-margin SVM} Maximising the margin $\max_{\w, b}\min_i t_i\,g(\x_i)/\norm{\w}$ thus becomes, after fixing the scale, maximising
$1/\norm{\w}$ subject to all functional margins being at least one --- equivalently minimising $\frac12\norm{\w}^2$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hardsvm}
  \min_{\w, b}\ \tfrac12 \norm{\w}^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) \ge 1, \quad i = 1, \dots, n .
\end{equation}
Replacing the normalisation $\min_i t_i\,g(\x_i) = 1$ by the constraints $t_i\,g(\x_i) \ge 1$ for all $i$ changes
nothing at the optimum: if every constraint held strictly, $t_i\,g(\x_i) > 1$, we could shrink $(\w, b)$ by a factor
slightly below one, stay feasible and reduce $\norm{\w}$; so at the optimum at least one constraint is active, which is
the normalisation we wanted. Fixing the scale by $\norm{\w} = 1$ instead gives a third equivalent problem, maximising the
smallest functional margin over unit vectors $\w$. All three formulations give the same hyperplane $H$ and differ only in
the length of $\w$.

This is a \emph{quadratic programme}: a convex quadratic objective with linear constraints (Note~7, Section~6), with a
unique solution if the data are separable.

\subsection{Lagrange multipliers, the dual and support vectors}\label{sec:svmdual}

The constraints of \eqref{eq:hardsvm} are inequalities, not the equalities of the Lagrange multipliers met in calculus,
so the method needs one more idea. Attach a multiplier $\alpha_i \ge 0$ to each constraint (we reuse the letter $\alpha$
of the dual perceptron \eqref{eq:dualw} on purpose: the two play the same role) and form the Lagrangian
\[
  L(\w, b, \alpha) = \tfrac12\norm{\w}^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\big[t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) - 1\big] .
\]
For a feasible $(\w, b)$ every bracket is $\ge 0$, so with $\alpha_i \ge 0$ we have $L \le \frac12\norm{\w}^2$. Hence, for
every $\alpha \ge 0$, the minimum of $L$ over \emph{all} $(\w, b)$ is a lower bound on the optimal value of
\eqref{eq:hardsvm}; the \emph{dual problem} asks for the largest such bound, which is why it is a maximisation over
$\alpha$. For a convex quadratic programme like this one the best bound is attained: dual and primal optima are equal.
The minimum over $(\w, b)$ is where the derivatives vanish,
\[
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial \w} = \w - \sum_i \alpha_i t_i\x_i = \vect{0} \;\Rightarrow\; \w = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i t_i\,\x_i ,
  \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -\sum_i \alpha_i t_i = 0 .
\]
To substitute back, use $\sum_i \alpha_i t_i\,\w\T\x_i = \w\T\big(\sum_i \alpha_i t_i\x_i\big) = \w\T\w$ and
$\sum_i \alpha_i t_i\,b = 0$:
\[
  L = \tfrac12\w\T\w - \w\T\w - 0 + \sum_i \alpha_i = \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\Big\|\sum_i \alpha_i t_i\x_i\Big\|^2 ,
\]
and expanding the squared norm gives the \emph{dual problem}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:svmdual}
  \max_{\alpha}\ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_i\alpha_j t_i t_j\,\x_i\T\x_j
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad \alpha_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_i \alpha_i t_i = 0 .
\end{equation}
At the optimum, the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) conditions add \emph{complementary slackness},
\[
  \alpha_i\,\big[t_i\,g(\x_i) - 1\big] = 0 \qquad \text{for every } i .
\]
It follows from the equality of the two optima: at the solution $L = \frac12\norm{\w}^2 - \sum_i
\alpha_i[t_i\,g(\x_i) - 1]$ must equal $\frac12\norm{\w}^2$, so the sum vanishes, and since each of its terms is $\ge 0$,
each term vanishes. Either an example lies exactly on the margin ($t_i\,g(\x_i) = 1$) or its multiplier vanishes: a
constraint that is not tight could be removed without changing the solution, so it exerts no ``force'' on it. The examples
with $\alpha_i > 0$ are the \emph{support vectors}: $\w$ is a combination of them alone, and moving any other point,
without crossing the margin, changes nothing. The bias follows from any support vector $s$: multiplying $t_s(\w\T\x_s + b) = 1$ by $t_s$ gives $b = t_s - \w\T\x_s$.
Like the dual perceptron \eqref{eq:dualw}, the dual SVM needs the data only through inner products $\x_i\T\x_j$, which is
what makes the kernel SVM of Note~10, Section~7, possible. In practice the $\alpha_i$ are found numerically: the
dual-coordinate-ascent algorithm given there also works with the linear kernel $k(\x, \vect{z}) = \x\T\vect{z}$, for the
slightly modified problem in which the bias is absorbed and penalised, so that the constraint $\sum_i\alpha_it_i = 0$ drops
out (see also the running-time box below). Here we find them by hand, by guessing the support vectors and checking.

\begin{worked}{the maximum-margin line by hand}
Take the four points of the perceptron trace: positives $\x_1 = (1, 2)$, $\x_3 = (3, 1)$; negatives $\x_2 = (0, 0)$,
$\x_4 = (2, 0)$.\\[0.3em]
\emph{0. A first guess that fails.} The closest pair of opposite points is $\x_3, \x_4$. With only them on the margin,
$\w$ is parallel to $\x_3 - \x_4 = (1, 1)$, say $\w = k(1, 1)$, and $g(\x_3) = 4k + b = 1$, $g(\x_4) = 2k + b = -1$ give
$k = 1$, $b = -3$. But then $g(\x_1) = 1 + 2 - 3 = 0 < 1$: the constraint of $\x_1$ is violated, so $\x_1$ must be on the
margin too.\\[0.3em]
\emph{1. Guess the support vectors.} The closest point of the negative class to the segment joining the positives is
$\x_4$; we guess that $\x_1$ and $\x_3$ lie on $g = +1$ and $\x_4$ on $g = -1$.\\[0.3em]
\emph{2. Solve.} Since $\x_1$ and $\x_3$ are both on $g = +1$, $\w$ is perpendicular to $\x_3 - \x_1 = (2, -1)$, so
$\w = k(1, 2)$. The conditions $g(\x_1) = 5k + b = 1$ and $g(\x_4) = 2k + b = -1$ give $k = \frac23$, $b = -\frac73$:
\[
  \w = \big(\tfrac23, \tfrac43\big), \qquad b = -\tfrac73, \qquad g(\x) = \tfrac13(2x_1 + 4x_2 - 7) .
\]
\emph{3. Check feasibility.} $g(\x_3) = \frac13(6 + 4 - 7) = 1$ and $t_2\,g(\x_2) = \frac73 \ge 1$: all constraints hold,
and $\x_2$ is not on the margin.\\[0.3em]
\emph{4. Check optimality.} Solve $\w = \sum_i \alpha_i t_i\x_i$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i t_i = 0$ with $\alpha_2 = 0$:
$\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 = \alpha_4$ and $\alpha_1(1, 2) + \alpha_3(3, 1) - \alpha_4(2, 0) = (\frac23, \frac43)$ give
$\alpha_1 = \frac29$, $\alpha_3 = \frac89$, $\alpha_4 = \frac{10}{9}$. All are positive, so the KKT conditions hold and the
guess was right; a negative $\alpha$ would have told us to drop that point from the guess.\\[0.3em]
\emph{Result.} $\norm{\w} = \frac{2\sqrt5}{3}$, the margin is $1/\norm{\w} \approx 0.67$ and the band width $2/\norm{\w} =
3/\sqrt5 \approx 1.34$; the dual
objective $\sum\alpha_i - \frac12\norm{\w}^2 = \frac{20}{9} - \frac{10}{9}$ equals the primal $\frac12\norm{\w}^2 = \frac{10}{9}$.
The perceptron's line $x_2 = 1/3$ kept only $1/3$ from the nearest points; the SVM keeps $0.67$ on each side
(Figure~\ref{fig:margin}).
\end{worked}

\subsection{Soft margin and the hinge loss}

For data that are not separable \eqref{eq:hardsvm} has no solution. \emph{Slack} variables $\xi_i \ge 0$ allow violations
at a price:
\[
  \min_{\w, b, \xi}\ \tfrac12\norm{\w}^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \quad\text{subject to}\quad t_i(\w\T\x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,
  \quad \xi_i \ge 0 .
\]
An example with $0 < \xi_i < 1$ is inside the margin but correct, one with $\xi_i = 1$ lies on the boundary, and one with
$\xi_i > 1$ is misclassified. For fixed
$(\w, b)$ the best slack is the smallest allowed one, $\xi_i = \max\big(0, 1 - t_i\,g(\x_i)\big)$. Substituting it and
dividing by $C$ gives an unconstrained problem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:softsvm}
  \min_{\w, b}\ \sum_{i=1}^n \max\big(0,\, 1 - t_i(\w\T\x_i + b)\big) + \frac{1}{2C}\norm{\w}^2 ,
\end{equation}
the hinge loss of Note~4 plus a ridge penalty: the SVM is regularised empirical risk minimisation, like everything else in
this course. The hyperparameter $C$ trades margin against training errors: small $C$ means strong regularisation, a wide
margin and many support vectors; large $C$ fits the training data harder; $C \to \infty$ recovers the hard margin. In the
dual, the only change is the box constraint $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$. Support vectors are now all points with
$t_i\,g(\x_i) \le 1$: those on the margin ($0 \le \alpha_i \le C$) and those inside it or misclassified ($\alpha_i = C$),
however far on the wrong side. The bias is computed from a support vector with $0 < \alpha_s < C$, which lies exactly on
the margin. Soft-margin SVMs, linear or kernelised, were among the most widely used
classifiers of the 2000s. They also extend to regression, with an $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss that ignores errors
smaller than $\varepsilon$ (support vector regression, Note~10, Section~8).

\begin{worked}{slacks and the role of $C$}
Keep the four points of the hand-solved example and add $\x_a = (1.5, 1.5)$ with $t_a = +1$ and $\x_b = (0, 3)$ with $t_b =
-1$. For the line found there, $g(\x) = \frac13(2x_1 + 4x_2 - 7)$, the functional margins $t_i\,g(\x_i)$ and slacks
$\xi_i = \max(0, 1 - t_i\,g(\x_i))$ are
\begin{center}\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
point & $\x_1$ & $\x_2$ & $\x_3$ & $\x_4$ & $\x_a$ & $\x_b$ \\
\midrule
$t_i\,g(\x_i)$ & $1$ & $\frac73$ & $1$ & $1$ & $\frac23$ & $-\frac53$ \\
$\xi_i$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $\frac13$ & $\frac83$ \\
status & on margin & beyond & on margin & on margin & inside, correct & misclassified \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
so $\sum_i\xi_i = 3$ and, with $\frac12\norm{\w}^2 = \frac{10}{9}$, the objective is $\frac{10}{9} + 3C$. Halving $(\w, b)$
keeps the same line but doubles the band: $\frac12\norm{\w}^2$ drops to $\frac{5}{18}$, while all margins halve, the slacks
become $\frac12, 0, \frac12, \frac12, \frac23, \frac{11}{6}$ and their sum grows to $4$, giving $\frac{5}{18} + 4C$. The
wide band is better when $C < \frac56$ (at $C = 0.1$: $0.68$ against $1.41$) and the narrow one when $C > \frac56$ (at $C =
10$: $31.1$ against $40.3$): small $C$ buys a wide margin at the price of slack, large $C$ does the opposite.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Training by subgradient descent}

The objective \eqref{eq:softsvm} is convex but has a kink where $t_i\,g(\x_i) = 1$, where no gradient exists. A
\emph{subgradient} of a convex function $J$ at $\w$ is any vector $\vect{v}$ with $J(\w') \ge J(\w) + \vect{v}\T(\w'
- \w)$ for all $\w'$: the plane with slope $\vect{v}$ touches the graph at $\w$ and stays below it. Where $J$ is
differentiable the only subgradient is the gradient; at the kink of the hinge any convex combination of the gradients of
the two pieces qualifies, and we pick the flat piece's. Gradient descent with subgradients still converges if the step
size decreases. To keep the algorithm short we use the bias trick, $\wt = (b, \w)$ and $\xt_i = (1, \x_i)$, and write the
objective as $\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\wt}^2 + \frac1n\sum_i \max(0, 1 - t_i\,\wt\T\xt_i)$ with $\lambda = 1/(nC)$. This
also penalises the bias slightly, a simple option discussed in the Pegasos paper; with a large separate step on an unpenalised bias the
iterates drift and converge very slowly. A subgradient of the term of example $i$ is
\[
  \lambda\wt - \ind{t_i\,\wt\T\xt_i < 1}\, t_i\xt_i .
\]
Points beyond the margin contribute only the shrinkage $\lambda\wt$; a point inside the margin or misclassified also pulls
$\wt$ towards $t_i\xt_i$ --- a perceptron update that fires already when $t_i\,g(\x_i) < 1$ instead of $\le 0$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pegasos} is the stochastic version, known as Pegasos~\cite{shalevshwartz2007}, with the step
$\eta_k = 1/(\lambda k)$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Soft-margin linear SVM by stochastic subgradient descent (Pegasos-style).}\label{alg:pegasos}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require augmented examples $(\xt_i, t_i)$, $\xt_i = (1, \x_i)$, $t_i \in \{-1, +1\}$; $\lambda = 1/(nC)$; number of steps $T$
\State $\wt \gets \vect{0}$ \Comment{$\wt = (b, \w)$: the bias is shrunk like the weights}
\For{$k = 1, \dots, T$}
  \State pick $i$ uniformly at random; \ $\eta \gets 1/(\lambda k)$
  \If{$t_i\,\wt\T\xt_i < 1$} \Comment{inside the margin or wrong}
    \State $\wt \gets (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\wt + \eta\,t_i\xt_i$
  \Else
    \State $\wt \gets (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\wt$ \Comment{only the ridge shrinkage}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\wt$ \Comment{in practice, often the average of the last iterates}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At $k = 1$ the shrinkage factor $1 - \eta\lambda$ is zero, so the first step simply sets $\wt = \eta\,t_i\xt_i$ whatever
the example; this is expected, not an error. (The basic Pegasos algorithm has no bias term; absorbing it with the bias trick is
the simplest way to add one.)

\begin{worked}{two Pegasos steps}
On the four points of the perceptron trace take $\lambda = 1$ (large, to keep the numbers simple). Step $k = 1$, $\eta = 1$,
visits $\x_1$, $\xt_1 = (1, 1, 2)$: its margin is $0 < 1$, so $\wt = (1 - 1)\,\vect{0} + 1 \cdot \xt_1 = (1, 1, 2)$, i.e.\
$b = 1$, $\w = (1, 2)$. Step $k = 2$, $\eta = \frac12$, visits $\x_4$, $\xt_4 = (1, 2, 0)$, $t_4 = -1$: its margin is
$-(1 + 2) = -3 < 1$, so $\wt = \frac12(1, 1, 2) - \frac12(1, 2, 0) = (0, -0.5, 1)$, i.e.\ $b = 0$, $\w = (-0.5, 1)$. The
first factor $(1 - \eta\lambda)$ shrinks the old weights, the second term is a perceptron-like push; with the steps
shrinking like $1/k$, later examples move $\wt$ less and less.
\end{worked}

\subsection{More than two classes}

Binary classifiers extend in two standard ways (Algorithm~\ref{alg:multiclass}). \emph{One-versus-rest} (OvR) trains $m$
classifiers, each separating one class from all the others, and predicts the class with the highest score.
\emph{One-versus-one} (OvO) trains $m(m-1)/2$ classifiers, one for each pair of classes on the data of those two classes
only, and lets them vote. With raw yes/no answers there are \emph{ambiguous regions}, where several or no classifiers
claim a point. Taking the highest OvR score removes them (up to exact ties). OvO voting shrinks them but can still tie:
``1 beats 2, 2 beats 3, 3 beats 1'' gives every class one vote; such ties are usually broken by summing the pairwise
scores. Figure~\ref{fig:ovr} shows the regions for three classes. Softmax regression (Section~\ref{sec:logistic}) needs
neither scheme: it is multiclass from the start. The same is true of the multiclass SVM of Crammer and
Singer~\cite{crammer2001}, which trains all $m$ score functions jointly by minimising the multiclass hinge loss
$\max\big(0, 1 + \max_{k \ne t} g_k(\x) - g_t(\x)\big)$ of Note~4, Section~2, plus a ridge penalty.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/n9_ovr.pdf}
\caption{Three classes, one-versus-rest with linear scores $g_c$. (a) Read as yes/no answers, the three lines $g_c = 0$
leave dark regions claimed by two classes and a central triangle claimed by none. (b) Taking the highest score assigns every
point to exactly one class; the boundaries are where two scores are equal.}
\label{fig:ovr}
\end{figure}

\begin{worked}{the two schemes can disagree}
With $m = 3$, a point gets OvR scores $g_1 = 0.8$, $g_2 = 0.3$, $g_3 = -1.1$: as yes/no answers classifiers 1 and 2 both
claim it (an ambiguous region), but the highest score picks class~1. The OvO scores $g_{12} = +0.2$ (1 beats 2),
$g_{13} = -0.4$ (3 beats 1), $g_{23} = -0.5$ (3 beats 2) give class~3 two votes: the schemes, trained on different data,
need not agree.
\end{worked}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{One-versus-rest and one-versus-one.}\label{alg:multiclass}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $\{(\x_i, t_i)\}$ with classes $1, \dots, m$; a binary learner \textsc{Bin} returning a score function
\Function{TrainOvR}{data} \Comment{predict $\argmax_c g_c(\x)$}
  \For{$c = 1, \dots, m$}
    \State $g_c \gets$ \Call{Bin}{all $\x_i$, label $+1$ if $t_i = c$, else $-1$}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\{g_c\}$
\EndFunction
\Function{TrainOvO}{data}
  \For{each pair $c < c'$}
    \State $g_{cc'} \gets$ \Call{Bin}{only the $\x_i$ of class $c$ (label $+1$) and $c'$ (label $-1$)}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\{g_{cc'}\}$
\EndFunction
\Function{PredictOvO}{$\x$}
  \State $v_c \gets$ number of pairs won by $c$ \Comment{$g_{cc'}(\x) > 0$: a win for $c$, else for $c'$}
  \State \Return $\argmax_c v_c$ \Comment{ties: largest sum of pairwise scores}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{runtime}{linear SVM and multiclass schemes}
\emph{Subgradient SVM (Pegasos)}: $O(d)$ per step, independent of $n$. For a fixed regularisation strength $\lambda$,
reaching accuracy $\epsilon$ on the objective takes about $1/(\lambda\epsilon)$ steps up to logarithmic
factors~\cite{shalevshwartz2007}, a cost $O(d/(\lambda\epsilon))$ that does not depend on $n$; with $\lambda = 1/(nC)$ at fixed
$C$ it is $O(dnC/\epsilon)$, linear in $n$ and with no $n \times n$ matrix. Memory $O(d)$; prediction $O(d)$. \emph{Dual
solvers}: generic quadratic-programming solvers form the $n \times n$ matrix of inner products, $O(n^2 d)$ time and
$O(n^2)$ memory; decomposition methods such as SMO compute its rows on demand with a cache and take between $O(n^2)$ and
$O(n^3)$ time in practice --- fine for $n$ up to some $10^4$--$10^5$, impractical for millions. Kernel SVMs (Note~10) must
work this way, but a \emph{linear} SVM need not: dual coordinate descent keeps $\w = \sum_i \alpha_i t_i\x_i$ up to date
at $O(nd)$ per epoch with no Gram matrix (the method behind the LIBLINEAR library). \emph{OvR}: $m$ binary trainings on all
$n$ points, prediction $O(md)$, memory $O(md)$. \emph{OvO}: $m(m-1)/2$ trainings on about $2n/m$ points each, prediction
$O(m^2 d)$, memory $O(m^2 d)$. For a learner linear in $n$, both total about $mn$ example visits; for a learner quadratic in
$n$, such as a dual QP solver, OvO's smaller problems make it the cheaper scheme.
\end{runtime}

\section{Limits of linear classifiers}\label{sec:features}\label{sec:limits}

Whatever the algorithm, a linear classifier draws a hyperplane, and some problems have no good hyperplane at all. The
classic example is the XOR pattern: the points $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, with equal coordinates, in class $t = +1$, and
$(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ in class $t = -1$ (the same convention as Note~10, Section~1). Suppose
$g(\x) = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b$ separated them, positive on the first pair and negative on the second:
\[
  b > 0, \qquad w_1 + w_2 + b > 0, \qquad w_2 + b < 0, \qquad w_1 + b < 0 .
\]
Adding the first two inequalities gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0$, adding the last two gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b < 0$: a
contradiction. No line separates XOR, and no amount of training --- perceptron, logistic or SVM --- will change that; the
perceptron simply never stops. Minsky and Papert's 1969 analysis of what single-layer perceptrons cannot compute (XOR is
the simplest case of their parity results) is often credited with contributing to a decline of neural-network research
in the 1970s~\cite{minsky1969}. Real data show the same defect in less obvious forms --- a signal region surrounded by
background, a class split into two islands, a curved boundary: the capacity of the hyperplane family (Note~4, Section~5)
is too small and the classifier underfits, however much data it is given.

The remedy keeps the losses, the optimisation and the margins, and changes the space in which the hyperplane is drawn:
adding a single product feature makes XOR separable (Note~10, Section~1), and Question~B7 asks for another such
feature. Such features can be chosen
explicitly (from physics, or as polynomials), implicitly through a kernel that replaces the inner products of the dual
forms of Sections~\ref{sec:dualperc} and~\ref{sec:svmdual}, or learned with the classifier, as in a neural network.
Explicit feature maps and the kernel trick are the subject of Note~10; learned features are where week~5 begins.

\begin{physics}{feature engineering, the physicist's way}
The invariant mass of a pair of photons, $m_{\gamma\gamma}^2 = 2E_1E_2(1 - \cos\theta_{12})$, is a non-linear function of
the measured energies and angles. In it, the signal of a Higgs boson decaying to two photons becomes a narrow peak on a
smooth background, so a mass window keeps most of the signal while removing most of the background --- something no single
hyperplane in the raw energies and angles could do. Physics has always supplied such
features. When deep networks were given only low-level detector quantities in benchmark searches for new particles, they
matched or exceeded the performance obtained with the best hand-engineered variables, having learned equivalent features
themselves~\cite{baldi2014}.
\end{physics}

\begin{keyidea}[Summary]
A linear classifier predicts $\sign(\w\T\x + b)$; its boundary is a hyperplane normal to $\w$, $g(\x)/\norm{\w}$ is a
signed distance, and $t\,g(\x)$ is the margin of an example. All the algorithms of this note learn hyperplanes, chosen by different
losses of the margin; least squares is pulled by distant easy points. The perceptron adds misclassified examples to $\w$, each update raising that example's
margin by $\norm{\x_i}^2 + 1$; it converges after at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ mistakes on separable data, stops at any separator,
and in dual form needs only inner products. Logistic regression gives probabilities, with gradient $\sum(y_i - t_i)\xt_i$
and a convex loss minimised by gradient descent or Newton/IRLS; softmax extends it to $m$ classes. The SVM maximises the
margin $1/\norm{\w}$ (band width $2/\norm{\w}$), depends only on its support vectors, and in soft-margin form is hinge loss plus ridge. No hyperplane
solves XOR: non-linear features are needed (Note~10).
\end{keyidea}

\begin{questions}
\begin{conceptual}
\item What does it mean geometrically that $\w$ is normal to the decision boundary? What happens to the boundary when $b$
is increased with $\w$ fixed, and what happens to the classifier when both $\w$ and $b$ are multiplied by $3$? By $-1$?
\item Nearest centroid and LDA both give linear boundaries. What information about the classes does LDA use that nearest
centroid ignores, and when do the two coincide?
\item Explain, using the shape of the squared loss as a function of the margin, why a cluster of far-away but correctly
classified points can make the least-squares classifier worse. Why are logistic regression and the SVM much less
affected?
\item Why can the training error rate not be minimised by gradient descent? What is a surrogate loss, and which surrogate
does each of the perceptron, logistic regression and the SVM use?
\item True or false, with a justification: ``after a perceptron update on example $i$, example $i$ is always classified
correctly.'' What does always hold after the update?
\item Describe in words and with a sketch what a perceptron update does to $\w$ and to the decision boundary when a
positive example is misclassified. Why can an update spoil a point that was previously correct?
\item On separable data the perceptron stops. Is the hyperplane it returns unique? Does it depend on the order in which
the examples are visited? Why might it generalise poorly?
\item What happens when the perceptron is run on non-separable data? Describe two remedies and what each returns.
\item In the dual form of the perceptron, what does $\alpha_i$ count, which examples have $\alpha_i = 0$, and why is this
form the starting point of kernel methods?
\item Logistic regression has ``regression'' in its name. In what sense does it regress, and why is it nevertheless a
linear classifier?
\item Why do the weights of an unregularised logistic regression grow without bound on separable data? What is wrong with
the resulting probabilities, and how is it fixed?
\item Compare gradient descent and Newton's method for logistic regression: cost per iteration, number of iterations, and
when you would choose each.
\item What are support vectors? Explain why the soft-margin SVM solution does not change when a correctly classified point
outside the margin is moved, as long as it stays outside the margin. Which group of points in
Figure~\ref{fig:linclass} (think of the distant amber cluster) has no influence on the linear SVM, and why does the same
group change the least-squares line?
\item What is the role of $C$ in the soft-margin SVM? Describe the solution for very small and very large $C$. The
perceptron criterion and the hinge loss are both zero for some correct examples: what does the ``1'' in $\max(0, 1 - z)$
change?
\item With $m = 10$ classes, how many binary classifiers do one-versus-rest and one-versus-one train, and on how many
examples each? What is an ambiguous region? How does each scheme resolve it, and can OvO voting still end in a tie?
\item Explain why no linear classifier can solve XOR, and why training longer, or with a different linear algorithm, does
not help. Name the three ways of obtaining non-linear features.
\item Why does Algorithm~\ref{alg:pegasos} use the bias trick instead of updating an unpenalised bias $b$ with the same
step $\eta_k = 1/(\lambda k)$? How large is that step in the first iterations when $\lambda = 1/(nC)$?
\end{conceptual}
